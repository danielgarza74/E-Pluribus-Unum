1861A letter

General Winfield Scott, head of the U.S. Army

Scott was 74. He wrote this to a general in Ohio, weeks after Fort Sumter, to explain how the North could win.

“We rely greatly on the sure operation of a complete blockade of the Atlantic and Gulf ports soon to commence. In connection with such blockade we propose a powerful movement down the Mississippi to the ocean, with a cordon of posts at proper points . . . the object being to clear out and keep open this great line of communication in connection with the strict blockade of the sea-board, so as to envelop the insurgent States and bring them to terms with less bloodshed than by any other plan.”
Winfield Scott to George B. McClellan, May 3, 1861, from The War of the Rebellion: Official Records, Series I, vol. 51, part 1; reprinted in American Heritage, Spring 2011. Spelling and wording unchanged.
Word help: blockade = ships that stop trade · cordon = a line of guards · insurgent = rebelling · envelop = surround · bring them to terms = make them give in

1861A court opinion

Roger B. Taney, Chief Justice of the Supreme Court

John Merryman was jailed in Maryland by the army without a trial. The Chief Justice said the President had no right to do this.

“I had supposed it to be one of those points of constitutional law, upon which there was no difference of opinion, and that it was admitted on all hands that the privilege of the writ could not be suspended except by act of Congress. When the conspiracy of which Aaron Burr was the head became so formidable . . . as to justify, in Mr. Jefferson’s opinion, the suspension of the writ, he claimed, on his part, no power to suspend it, but communicated his opinion to Congress . . . .”
Roger B. Taney, opinion in Ex parte Merryman, 1861; Teaching American History (Ashbrook Center), from The Merryman Habeas Corpus Case (Baltimore, 1861). Wording unchanged.
Word help: writ = a court order (here, one that frees a prisoner) · suspended = put on hold · privilege = special right · conspiracy = secret plot · formidable = dangerous · Congress = the lawmakers

1864A petition

Mayor James Calhoun and two Atlanta councilmen

Sherman ordered everyone to leave Atlanta. The city’s leaders begged him to change his mind.

“Many poor women are in advanced state of pregnancy; others now having young children, and whose husbands, for the greater part, are either in the army, prisoners, or dead. . . . how is it possible for the people still here (mostly women and children) to find any shelter? And how can they live through the winter in the woods? . . . what has this helpless people done, that they should be driven from their homes to wander strangers and outcasts and exiles, and to subsist on charity?”
James M. Calhoun, E. E. Rawson, and S. C. Wells to William T. Sherman, September 11, 1864; Civil War Notebook, from the Official Records, Series I, vol. 39, part 2. Wording unchanged.
Word help: advanced state = very late in pregnancy · shelter = a safe place to stay · helpless = unable to protect themselves · outcasts = people forced out · subsist = barely live · charity = help given freely

1864A letter

General William T. Sherman

This is Sherman’s answer to the Atlanta petition, written the next day.

“I have your letter of the 11th, in the nature of a petition to revoke my orders removing all the inhabitants from Atlanta. I have read it carefully, and give full credit to your statements of the distress that will be occasioned, and yet shall not revoke my orders . . . . You cannot qualify war in harsher terms than I will. War is cruelty, and you cannot refine it; and those who brought war into our country deserve all the curses and maledictions a people can pour out.”
William T. Sherman to James M. Calhoun et al., September 12, 1864; Wikisource, from Sherman’s Memoirs. Wording unchanged.
Word help: revoke = cancel · distress = suffering · qualify = describe · maledictions = curses · refine = make gentler
