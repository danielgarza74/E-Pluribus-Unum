PART 3SECTION CHECKS · 1.1–1.4

Section Checkpoints

Choose the best answer. A short reason appears when you get one right.

2.1 Why did Jefferson and Madison argue that the National Bank was unconstitutional?
Jefferson used strict construction and the Tenth Amendment. Hamilton answered with the Necessary and Proper Clause and implied powers.
2.1 What did the “Dinner Table Bargain” of 1790 trade?
Madison let the assumption bill pass and Hamilton backed a southern capital. The deal showed sectional bargaining holding the Union together.
2.1 According to Figure 2.3, how did the Federalists and Democratic-Republicans differ about who should govern?
The table shows Federalists trusting the “best people” and fearing too much democracy, while Republicans trusted farmers and state governments.
2.1 Hamilton said 90% of American import duties came from British trade. How did that figure shape the Federalist view of foreign policy?
Because most government revenue came from British trade, Federalists believed war with Britain would wreck the economy, so they leaned toward Britain.
2.2 Why did President Jefferson face a “crisis of conscience” over the Louisiana Purchase?
Jefferson set aside strict construction for a pragmatic reason: waiting to pass an amendment might let Napoleon change his mind.
2.2 How did the Haitian Revolution help lead to the Louisiana Purchase?
Without profitable Saint-Domingue, Louisiana was useless to Napoleon, and he also needed cash for war with Britain.
2.2 Why did the Embargo Act of 1807 fail as a policy?
Britain and France found other markets, while American harbors filled with idle ships and farmers could not export their crops.
2.2 The Treaty of Ghent changed nothing on paper. Why did the Battle of New Orleans still doom the Federalist Party?
The victory flooded the nation with pride just as the Hartford delegates arrived with their demands, and some of them had even discussed secession. The Federalists never recovered.
2.3 What did Henry Clay’s Compromise Tariff of 1833 do to end the Nullification Crisis?
Both sides claimed victory, but the question of whether states could secede stayed unanswered until 1861.
2.3 What did the spread of property-free voting between 1820 and 1840 change about American politics?
The mass electorate turned politics into parades, barbecues, and rallies, and Jackson became its symbol.
2.3 What does Figure 2.14 show about “common man” democracy?
The table supports the text’s phrase “white man’s republic”: gains for some groups came with losses for others.
2.3 What happened after the Supreme Court ruled for the Cherokee in Worcester v. Georgia?
The ruling affirmed tribal sovereignty, but the Treaty of New Echota was ratified by a single vote and led to the Trail of Tears.

Two Leaders, Two Views of Federal Power

Read both sources, then answer in your own words.

“I consider the foundation of the Constitution as laid on this ground: That ‘all powers not delegated to the United States, by the Constitution, nor prohibited by it to the States, are reserved to the States or to the people.’ To take a single step beyond the boundaries thus specially drawn around the powers of Congress, is to take possession of a boundless field of power, no longer susceptible of any definition…”
Thomas Jefferson, Opinion on the Constitutionality of a National Bank, 1791. Text from the Avalon Project, Yale Law School.

President Andrew Jackson, Second Annual Message to Congress, December 6, 1830. National Archives, Milestone Documents.
