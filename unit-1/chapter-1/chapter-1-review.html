PART 3SECTION CHECKS · 1.1–1.4

Section Checkpoints

Choose the best answer. A short reason appears when you get one right.

1.1 After Bacon’s Rebellion in 1676, Virginia’s planters turned toward enslaved Africans as their main workforce. What best explains this shift?
The rebellion terrified the ruling elite. To prevent future class warfare, planters moved from unpredictable white indentured servants to a more permanent, controllable labor force.
1.1 Which statement best describes how New England governed itself in the 1600s?
The Town Meeting gave New England broad political participation for church members, but it also demanded conformity, as Roger Williams and Anne Hutchinson learned.
1.1 How did the First Great Awakening help prepare the colonies for independence?
If a common person could judge a minister’s soul, could they not also judge a political leader? The habit of dissent moved from religious meetings into town meetings.
1.2 Why did colonists reject the British theory of virtual representation?
Colonists believed in “actual representation.” The tax itself was low; the issue was power and consent.
1.2 Why did the shooting of March 5, 1770, become known as the “Boston Massacre”?
Patriot leaders turned a tragic confrontation into a masterpiece of propaganda. John Adams defended the soldiers, and most were acquitted.
1.2 Lord Dunmore’s Proclamation of November 1775 offered freedom to enslaved people who joined the British. What was its effect on Southern planters?
Planters such as George Washington were outraged. The Northern fight over liberty became tied to a Southern fight to protect slavery, which united the two regions.
1.2 Which change best shows colonists moving from “rights of Englishmen” to “natural rights”?
The Declaration of Independence rests on natural rights, the social contract, and the right of revolution, not on British history and tradition.
1.3 Why could the government under the Articles of Confederation not pay its debts or its soldiers?
Congress had no power to tax. It could only “requisition” funds, and Rhode Island even vetoed a modest 5% import tariff.
1.3 What was the lasting importance of the Northwest Ordinance of 1787?
New territories would enter the Union as equals once they reached 60,000 free inhabitants, and the ban on slavery set a precedent for federal regulation.
1.3 How did Shays’ Rebellion change the thinking of leaders such as George Washington?
The “spirit of ’76,” the fear of tyranny, gave way to the “spirit of ’87,” the fear of anarchy. Twelve states then sent delegates to Philadelphia.
1.4 The Great Compromise ended the deadlock between large and small states. What did it create?
Roger Sherman’s committee blended the Virginia Plan (population) and the New Jersey Plan (state equality) into a two-house Congress with a “split personality.”
1.4 Why did the Federalists promise that the first Congress would add a Bill of Rights?
Ratification was razor-thin: 187–168 in Massachusetts, 89–79 in Virginia, and three votes in New York. The promise tipped the balance.
1.4 In The Federalist, Madison argued that a large republic is safer than a small one. What was his reasoning?
Madison turned conventional wisdom upside down: many competing interests would check each other, and “ambition must be made to counteract ambition.”

PART 5CASE STUDY · PRIMARY SOURCES

Two Founders, Two Worries

Read both sources, then answer in your own words.

“We are either a United people, or we are not. If the former, let us, in all matters of general concern act as a nation, which have national objects to promote, and a national character to support. If we are not, let us no longer act a farce by pretending to it.”
George Washington to James Madison, November 30, 1785. Founders Online, National Archives.
“If men were angels, no government would be necessary. . . . In framing a government which is to be administered by men over men, the great difficulty lies in this: you must first enable the government to control the governed; and in the next place oblige it to control itself.”
James Madison (attributed), The Federalist No. 51, February 8, 1788. The Avalon Project, Yale Law School.
Word help: farce = a ridiculous pretend show · the governed = the people who live under a government · oblige = require
