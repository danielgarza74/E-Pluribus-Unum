1836A memoir of a strike

Lowell mill workers

Harriet Robinson worked in a Lowell mill as a girl. Here she remembers a strike when the owners cut wages.

“It was estimated that as many as twelve or fifteen hundred girls turned out, and walked in procession through the streets. They had neither flags nor music, but sang songs . . . . ‘Oh! I cannot be a slave, / I will not be a slave, / For I’m so fond of liberty / That I cannot be a slave.’”
Harriet Hanson Robinson, Loom and Spindle (1898), on the Lowell strike of October 1836; The American Yawp Reader. Robinson wrote this memory 62 years later.
Word help: turned out = went on strike · procession = a parade · operatives = factory workers · I cannot be a slave = I will not be controlled

1837An essay

Catharine Beecher, writer on women’s duty

Beecher wrote this to answer two women, Angelina and Sarah Grimké, who spoke in public against slavery.

“It is the grand feature of the Divine economy, that there should be different stations of superiority and subordination . . . . Heaven has appointed to one sex the superior, and to the other the subordinate station, and this without any reference to the character or conduct of either. It is therefore as much for the dignity as it is for the interest of females, in all respects to conform to the duties of this relation. . . . Woman is to win every thing by peace and love . . . .”
Catharine E. Beecher, An Essay on Slavery and Abolitionism, with Reference to the Duty of American Females (Philadelphia, 1837); excerpt from Teach US History (Ashbrook Center).
Word help: Divine economy = God’s plan · superiority = higher rank · subordinate = lower rank · conform = go along with · dignity = honor

1848Company rules

Hamilton Manufacturing Company, Lowell

A mill company printed these rules for its workers in a guidebook to Lowell.

“They are not to be absent from their work without the consent of the overseer, except in cases of sickness . . . . They are to board in one of the houses of the company . . . . All persons entering into the employment of the company are considered as engaged for twelve months . . . . The company will not employ anyone who is habitually absent from public worship on the Sabbath or known to be guilty of immorality.”
“Regulations to be observed by all persons employed by the Hamilton Manufacturing Company,” Handbook to Lowell (1848), pp. 42–44; Encyclopaedia Britannica, Primary Sources, “Factory life and rules at Lowell, Massachusetts, U.S.”
Word help: overseer = the boss of a work room · board = live and eat · engaged = hired · habitually = again and again · Sabbath = Sunday · immorality = bad behavior

1848A declaration

Elizabeth Cady Stanton and the Seneca Falls convention

Stanton and Lucretia Mott organized a women’s rights convention. Stanton wrote this paper, copying the Declaration of Independence.

“We hold these truths to be self-evident; that all men and women are created equal; that they are endowed by their Creator with certain inalienable rights . . . . The history of mankind is a history of repeated injuries and usurpations on the part of man toward woman, having in direct object the establishment of an absolute tyranny over her. . . . He has never permitted her to exercise her inalienable right to the elective franchise. He has compelled her to submit to laws, in the formation of which she had no voice.”
Declaration of Sentiments, Seneca Falls, New York, July 1848; National Park Service, Women’s Rights National Historical Park.
Word help: inalienable = cannot be taken away · usurpations = taking power that is not yours · tyranny = harsh, unfair rule · elective franchise = the right to vote
