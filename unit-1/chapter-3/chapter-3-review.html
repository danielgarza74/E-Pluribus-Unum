PART 3SECTION CHECKS · 1.1–1.4

Section Checkpoints

Choose the best answer. A short reason appears when you get one right.

3.1 How did the Erie Canal change life for northern farmers?
The canal cut the cost of shipping freight from Buffalo to New York City from $100 to $5 per ton, turning farm families into sellers in a national market.
3.1 What made the Lowell System different from the older putting-out system?
Lowell mills combined every step of cloth making and ran by the clock and the factory bell, which the text calls “time discipline.”
3.1 According to the chapter, how did abolitionism help lead to the women’s rights movement?
Mott and Stanton were made to sit behind a curtain at the 1840 World Anti-Slavery Convention. That showed them the parallel between enslaved people’s status and women’s status, and led to Seneca Falls in 1848.
3.2 How did the cotton gin change the future of slavery?
Before 1793 many leaders hoped slavery would wither away. After the gin, cotton became the most profitable crop in the Atlantic world and slavery became the foundation of the Southern economy.
3.2 Why did the internal slave trade grow after Congress banned the international slave trade in 1808?
With imports from Africa and the Caribbean closed, the demand for labor in the Deep South was met by forcing about one million people to move from the Upper South between 1790 and 1860.
3.2 Why did John C. Calhoun and others replace the “necessary evil” argument with the “positive good” argument?
As abolitionism grew, Southern leaders defended slavery as a good for society. That closed off the room for gradual change that “necessary evil” had left open.
3.3 What did John O’Sullivan mean by “Manifest Destiny”?
In 1845 O’Sullivan wrote of “our Manifest Destiny to overspread and to possess the whole of the continent.” The idea mixed national pride, religion, and a belief in racial hierarchy.
3.3 How did the Oregon dispute with Britain end under President Polk?
Polk did not want two wars at once, so he settled for the 49th parallel. Northern Democrats felt betrayed and suspected he fought only for land that could expand slavery.
3.3 What was the chapter’s explanation for why the Wilmot Proviso mattered even though it never became law?
Northern Whigs and Democrats voted for it and Southern Whigs and Democrats voted against it. It also forced one question on every new territory: will it be free or slave?
3.3 Why did California’s state constitution in 1849 exclude slavery?
The text says the decision came from the miners’ individualism and not from moral objection to slavery. California’s fast growth let it skip the territorial phase.
3.4 Why did the Fugitive Slave Act turn many indifferent Northerners against slavery?
The law denied accused runaways a jury trial and made citizens assist marshals. Many Northerners felt the “Slave Power” was reaching into their own communities.
3.4 What problem did popular sovereignty create in Kansas?
Pro-slavery “border ruffians” cast fraudulent votes and anti-slavery settlers armed themselves, producing two rival governments and the violence called Bleeding Kansas.
3.4 How did the Dred Scott decision convince many Northerners that the Court had become a tool of slaveholders?
Taney also said Black Americans were not citizens. By ruling that Congress could not ban slavery in the territories, the Court called the Republican Party’s central goal illegal.
3.4 Lincoln won the Election of 1860 with 180 electoral votes. What does the chapter say about his victory?
Lincoln carried every free state, but in ten Southern states his name was not even on the ballot. Many in the Deep South saw his election as the signal to leave the Union.

Two Visions: Destiny and Division

Read both sources, then answer in your own words.

“…the right of our manifest destiny to overspread and to possess the whole of the continent which Providence has given us for the development of the great experiment of liberty and federated self-government entrusted to us.”
John L. O’Sullivan, “Annexation,” The United States Magazine and Democratic Review, July–August 1845.
“A house divided against itself cannot stand. I believe this government cannot endure, permanently half slave and half free. I do not expect the Union to be dissolved—I do not expect the house to fall—but I do expect it will cease to be divided. It will become all one thing or all the other.”
Abraham Lincoln, “House Divided” Speech, Illinois Republican State Convention, June 16, 1858.
