UNIT 3

Industrialization and Reform

Chapter 8 opener photo. Photo: James St. John / Wikimedia Commons, CC BY 2.0

Unit question

Does the government have a duty to protect its citizens from the power of big business, and how did Americans respond to the rapid transformation of their society?

Between the completion of the transcontinental railroad and the presidency of Woodrow Wilson, the United States became an industrial, urban, and increasingly diverse nation, and it did so faster than its institutions could adjust. This unit asks whether government owed its citizens protection from the power of big business, and it follows the answers Americans gave: some by organizing, some by protesting, and some by legislating.

Chapter 8 traces the railroads, steel, oil, and corporate strategies that built a national market, and the workers who paid for it, from Haymarket to Pullman. Chapter 9 turns to the people and places that industry drew together: immigrants processed at Ellis Island and Angel Island, nativism and exclusion, crowded tenements, political machines such as Tammany Hall, and reformers such as Jane Addams. Chapter 10 then shows farmers, middle-class reformers, and presidents using organization, protest, and government power in response, from the Omaha Platform to the Square Deal and the New Freedom, and asks what their reforms left unfinished.

Students who complete the unit should be able to explain how industrial growth concentrated wealth and power, why cities and immigration made that power visible, and why Americans disagreed about the remedy. They should also be able to judge how fully reform answered the unit question, and for whom.

California standards alignment (unit abstract): 11.2 (Industrialization, Urbanization, Immigration, Populism, Progressivism)

California standards addressed

As printed on the chapter openers for this unit.

  • HSS-11.2Students analyze the relationship among the rise of industrialization, large-scale rural-to-urban migration, and massive immigration from Southern and Eastern Europe.
  • 11.2.1Know the effects of industrialization on living and working conditions, including the portrayal of working conditions and food safety in Upton Sinclair’s The Jungle.
  • 11.2.5Discuss corporate mergers that produced trusts and cartels and the economic and political policies of industrial leaders.
  • 11.2.6Trace the economic development of the United States and its emergence as a major industrial power, including its gains from trade and the advantages of its physical geography.
  • 11.2.2Describe the changing landscape, including the growth of cities linked by industry and trade, and the development of cities divided according to race, ethnicity, and class.
  • 11.2.3Trace the effect of the Americanization movement.
  • 11.2.4Analyze the effect of urban political machines and responses to them by immigrants and middle-class reformers.
  • 11.2.7Analyze the similarities and differences between the ideologies of Social Darwinism and Social Gospel (e.g., using biographies of William Graham Sumner, Billy Sunday, Dwight L. Moody).
  • 11.2.8Examine the effect of political programs and activities of Populists.
  • 11.2.9Understand the effect of political programs and activities of the Progressives (e.g., federal regulation of railroad transport, Children’s Bureau, the Sixteenth Amendment, Theodore Roosevelt, Hiram Johnson).
  • RH.11-12.7Integrate and evaluate multiple sources of information presented in diverse formats and media (e.g., visually, quantitatively, as well as in words) in order to address a question or solve a problem.
