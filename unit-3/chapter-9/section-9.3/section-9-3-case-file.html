1883A federal law

Congress of the United States

Reformers wanted to stop the spoils system, where jobs went to loyal party workers. Congress passed this law in 1883.

“An act to regulate and improve the civil service of the United States. . . . First, for open, competitive examinations for testing the fitness of applicants for the public service . . . . no person in the public service is for that reason under any obligations to contribute to any political fund . . . .”
Civil Service Reform (Pendleton) Act, January 16, 1883; National Archives, Milestone Documents. Used here in short pieces.
Word help: civil service = government jobs · regulate = control with rules · competitive examinations = tests that anyone can take · fitness = being able to do the job · political fund = money given to a party

1898A magazine article

Jane Addams, founder of Hull House

Addams lived and worked in a Chicago neighborhood. Here she explains why voters liked the local ward boss, called the Alderman.

“The Alderman, therefore, bails out his constituents when they are arrested . . . . An Italian laborer wants a job more than anything else, and quite simply votes for the man who promises him one. . . . The Alderman gives presents at weddings and christenings. . . . The Alderman is really elected because he is a good friend and neighbor. . . . If we would hold to our political democracy, some pains must be taken to keep on common ground . . . .”
Jane Addams, “Why the Ward Boss Rules,” The Outlook, April 2, 1898, 879–82; Macmillan Learning document reader. Used here in short pieces.
Word help: bails out = pays to get someone out of jail · constituents = voters who live in his area · christenings = baptism ceremonies for babies · democracy = government by the people · common ground = a shared place or idea

1905A set of “plain talks”

George Washington Plunkitt, Tammany ward boss

Plunkitt was a Tammany Hall boss in New York. A reporter wrote down his “plain talks.” Here he describes his daily work.

“If there’s a fire in Ninth, Tenth, or Eleventh Avenue, for example, any hour of the day or night, I’m usually there with some of my election district captains as soon as the fire-engines. If a family is burned out I don’t ask whether they are Republicans or Democrats, and I don’t refer them to the Charity Organization Society, which would investigate their case in a month or two and decide they were worthy of help about the time they are dead from starvation. . . . It’s philanthropy, but it’s politics, too . . . .”
George Washington Plunkitt, in William L. Riordon, Plunkitt of Tammany Hall (New York, 1905), Chapter 6; National Humanities Center. Apostrophes and dashes cleaned from the online text. Used here in short pieces.
Word help: election district captains = Plunkitt’s helpers in each area · Charity Organization Society = a charity that checked each family first · investigate = look into · philanthropy = giving to help others

1907A book

Walter Rauschenbusch, Social Gospel minister

Rauschenbusch was a pastor who worked among poor families in New York. In this book he asks what Christians owe the people hurt by the new economy.

“As capitalism grows, it must create a proletariat to correspond. . . . Competitive commerce exalts selfishness to the dignity of a moral principle. . . . If money dominates, the ideal cannot dominate. If we serve mammon, we cannot serve the Christ. . . . Nations do not die by wealth, but by injustice. . . . In the last resort the only hope is in the moral forces which can be summoned to the rescue.”
Walter Rauschenbusch, Christianity and the Social Crisis (New York: Macmillan, 1907), 247–86; Teaching American History, Ashbrook Center. Used here in short pieces.
Word help: capitalism = an economy run by private owners for profit · proletariat = the working class · exalts = lifts up · mammon = the love of money · injustice = unfair treatment
