PART 3SECTION CHECKS · 1.1–1.4

Section Checkpoints

Choose the best answer. A short reason appears when you get one right.

9.1 Why did historians call the arrivals after 1870 the “New Immigrants”?
The earlier wave came from places like Great Britain, Germany, and Scandinavia. The New Immigrants were largely Catholic, Orthodox Christian, or Jewish, and spoke languages that sounded foreign to native-born Americans.
9.1 Figure 9.2 lists pogroms as a push factor for Jews in the Russian Empire. Which choice is a pull factor drawing them to America?
Push factors are bad conditions at home, such as pogroms and restrictive laws. Pull factors are the attractions of the destination, such as freedom and the hope that a child’s chances would not be limited by religion.
9.1 Figure 9.3 compares the two gateways. What difference does it show between Ellis Island and Angel Island?
Only about 2 percent were denied entry at Ellis Island, while Chinese arrivals at Angel Island faced hostile questioning and detention in barracks surrounded by barbed wire. The text says this showed different government attitudes toward Europeans and Asians.
9.1 What was the Chinese Exclusion Act of 1882 the first law to do?
It came after years of anti-Chinese pressure in California and banned almost all Chinese immigration for ten years. It was extended until 1943.
9.2 Why did skyscrapers become possible only after the steel-skeleton frame arrived?
Masonry walls had to be very thick at the base, so most buildings rarely passed five stories. With steel beams carrying the load, walls could be hung like curtains, and the safety elevator made tall buildings practical.
9.2 According to Section 9.2, how did electric trolleys change where Americans lived?
Trolleys were faster and cleaner than horse cars, so suburbs could grow. The text says this separated home from work and increasingly divided cities by social class and income.
9.2 The Tenement House Act of 1879 required a window in every bedroom, yet the dumbbell tenement still failed. What went wrong?
The shaft gave almost no light or air to the lower floors. Residents dumped garbage in it, and in a fire it sucked flames upward through the whole building.
9.2 Why did Jacob Riis’s photographs persuade many Americans when his written reports had not?
His written reports were dismissed as exaggerations. Magnesium flash powder let him photograph dark interiors, and How the Other Half Lives (1890) led to stricter housing laws.
9.3 Why did immigrants often support the political machine?
Precinct captains spoke the newcomers’ language and knew each family. In exchange for help, the machine got a loyal voting bloc through patronage.
9.3 How did Thomas Nast’s cartoons help bring down Boss Tweed?
Tweed said his constituents could not read but “can’t help seeing them damn pictures.” The Tweed Ring was broken in 1871, and Tweed died in a New York jail in 1878.
9.3 How did the Social Gospel differ from traditional charity?
Leaders such as Washington Gladden and Walter Rauschenbusch argued it was impossible to save a man’s soul while the industrial system crushed his body.
9.3 Figure 9.17 compares Plunkitt and Addams. What is their key difference in how they saw the city?
Plunkitt traded favors for votes and defended honest graft. Addams argued that clean air, safe schools, and fair wages were rights of citizens, not rewards for loyalty.
