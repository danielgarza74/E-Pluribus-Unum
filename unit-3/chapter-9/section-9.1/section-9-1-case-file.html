1878A public address

Denis Kearney, Workingmen’s Party of California

Kearney led a party of white workers in California. This address is full of prejudice against Chinese workers. Read it to see what the movement wanted.

“Our moneyed men have ruled us for the past thirty years. . . . These cheap slaves fill every place. . . . Every door is closed. . . . We are men, and propose to live like men in this free land, without the contamination of slave labor . . . . California must be all American or all Chinese.”
Denis Kearney and H. L. Knight, “Appeal from California. The Chinese Invasion. Workingmen’s Address,” Indianapolis Times, February 28, 1878; History Matters, George Mason University. Used here in short pieces.
Word help: moneyed men = rich people · cheap slaves = Kearney’s insulting name for low-paid Chinese workers · contamination = something that spoils or makes unclean · slave labor = forced or nearly unpaid work

1882A federal law

Congress of the United States

Congress passed this law four years after Kearney’s address. It was the first federal law to ban one national group.

“. . . until the expiration of ten years next after the passage of this act . . . it shall not be lawful for any Chinese laborer to come . . . to remain within the United States. . . . [Section 14:] . . . no State court or court of the United States shall admit Chinese to citizenship . . . .”
Chinese Exclusion Act, May 6, 1882, Sections 1 and 14; Avalon Project, Yale Law School, from the Statutes at Large. Used here in short pieces.
Word help: expiration = the end · lawful = allowed by law · laborer = a worker · admit = let in or accept · citizenship = full membership in a country

1883A poem

Emma Lazarus, poet

Lazarus wrote this poem on November 2, 1883. In the poem, the Statue of Liberty speaks.

“Keep, ancient lands, your storied pomp!” cries she
With silent lips. “Give me your tired, your poor,
Your huddled masses yearning to breathe free,
The wretched refuse of your teeming shore.
Send these, the homeless, tempest-tost to me,
I lift my lamp beside the golden door!”
Emma Lazarus, “The New Colossus,” written November 2, 1883 (lines 9–14); Poets.org, Academy of American Poets. Date from the National Park Service, Statue of Liberty National Monument.
Word help: storied pomp = famous show of power · huddled = crowded close together · wretched refuse = unwanted leftovers · teeming = crowded · tempest-tost = thrown about by storms

1885A letter

Saum Song Bo, a Chinese American

Saum Song Bo wrote this letter about the Statue of Liberty. It was first printed in the American Missionary in October 1885.

“I consider it as an insult to us Chinese to call on us to contribute . . . this country is the land of liberty for men of all nations except the Chinese. . . . That statue represents liberty holding a torch which lights the passage of those of all nations . . . . As for the Chinese who are here, are they allowed to enjoy liberty as men of all other nationalities enjoy it?”
Saum Song Bo, letter on the Statue of Liberty, American Missionary, October 1885; Center for Asian American Media, Separate Lives, Broken Dreams. Used here in short pieces.
Word help: insult = a rude or hurtful act · contribute = give money · represents = stands for · passage = a way through · nationalities = people from different countries
