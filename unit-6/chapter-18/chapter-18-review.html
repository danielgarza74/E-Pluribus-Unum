PART 3SECTION CHECKS · 1.1–1.4

Section Checkpoints

Choose the best answer. A short reason appears when you get one right.

18.1 Why did Charles Hamilton Houston’s plan start by suing over unequal facilities instead of asking the Court to overturn Plessy right away?
Houston’s “equalization” strategy used the South’s own “separate but equal” rhetoric against it. Equal duplicate facilities were prohibitively expensive, so segregation might become too costly to keep.
18.1 Why did Thurgood Marshall bring psychologists and sociologists, including the Clarks’ doll experiments, into the Brown case?
Marshall shifted the debate from physical disparities to the “intangible” effects of segregation, arguing it stamped Black children with a sense of inferiority.
18.1 How did many Southern politicians use the Brown II phrase “with all deliberate speed”?
The ambiguous phrase was meant to give communities time, but local politicians read it as an invitation to delay integration indefinitely. Massive Resistance followed, and the Southern Manifesto backed it.
18.1 Eisenhower had privately disagreed with Brown. Why did he still send the 101st Airborne to Little Rock?
As a former general, Eisenhower saw state forces defying a federal court as a constitutional crisis. It was the first time since Reconstruction that a President used federal troops to protect Black Southerners’ rights.
18.2 Why did the Montgomery Bus Boycott put real economic pressure on the city?
Because most of the riders were Black, the boycott pushed the transit company toward bankruptcy and hurt downtown merchants. It was finally won in federal court in Browder v. Gayle.
18.2 According to the chapter’s comparison, how did the SCLC differ from the NAACP?
Churches were the only institutions in the South entirely owned and controlled by Black citizens, so the SCLC built its campaign of mass action on them while the NAACP kept working in the courts.
18.2 What did the Freedom Riders hope to force the Kennedy administration to do?
The Supreme Court had banned segregation in interstate terminals in 1960, but states ignored it. After mob violence in Alabama, the federal government acted and the ICC issued enforceable orders in September 1961.
18.3 Why did the SCLC choose Birmingham for its 1963 campaign, Project C?
Project C stood for Confrontation. The SCLC wanted to provoke Connor into overreacting so that television images would outrage the nation and push Kennedy to act.
18.3 How did King answer the eight clergymen who called him an “outside agitator” in his Letter from Birmingham Jail?
King also argued that privileged groups rarely give up their privileges voluntarily and that citizens have a moral duty to disobey unjust laws while accepting the penalty.
18.3 What finally allowed the Senate to pass the Civil Rights Act of 1964?
Breaking a filibuster took a two-thirds cloture vote. Johnson’s “Johnson Treatment” won Dirksen’s support, and on June 10, 1964 the Senate broke a filibuster on a civil rights bill for the first time.
18.3 How did “Bloody Sunday” in Selma help lead to the Voting Rights Act of 1965?
The violence on the Edmund Pettus Bridge was broadcast nationally. Johnson then addressed Congress on television, and the law outlawed literacy tests and sent in federal examiners.
18.4 Why were the Civil Rights Act and the Voting Rights Act not enough for Black residents of Northern cities?
De jure segregation, which was segregation by law, had been destroyed. De facto segregation, kept in place by redlining, blockbusting, and white flight, was far harder to reach with a single federal law.
18.4 What did Stokely Carmichael’s “Black Power” slogan show about the movement in June 1966?
King pleaded with Carmichael to drop the phrase, fearing it would alienate white allies. SNCC later voted to expel its white members, and the unified coalition of the early 1960s split apart.
18.4 What did the Kerner Commission conclude about the urban riots of the mid-1960s?
The 1968 report warned of “two societies, one black, one white—separate and unequal” and urged major investment in housing, education, and jobs. The Johnson administration largely ignored it.
