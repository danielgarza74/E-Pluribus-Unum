AMERICAN CHRONICLES · CHAPTER 19 · THE SIXTIES AND THE CULTURAL REVOLUTION

The East L.A. Blowouts

The story of the March 1968 walkouts in which thousands of Mexican American students demanded an education that respected their language and history

← BACK TO CHAPTER 19

In the spring of 1968, the high schools of East Los Angeles looked, to the students who walked their halls, like places built to keep them in their place. Mexican American students made up about three of every four students in the area’s schools, yet the schools offered them crowded classrooms, very few counselors, and, for many, a steady push toward vocational and domestic classes instead of college preparation. At many of these campuses, dropout rates ran near or above fifty percent. Students were punished for speaking Spanish, and the curriculum said almost nothing about Mexican American history.

One of the few adults who listened was Sal Castro, a social studies teacher at Lincoln High School who taught Mexican American history and culture. When students told him of their frustration, Castro first urged them to take their grievances to the school board. When the board ignored them, he helped them prepare for something larger. The students called it a “blowout,” a sudden explosion of protest that would empty classrooms across the city.

The blowouts began unevenly. On March 1, students at Wilson High School walked out in an unplanned protest. On March 5, about two thousand students at Garfield High School left their classes, and administrators called the police. On March 6, students walked out at Roosevelt, Lincoln, and Belmont High Schools, even though administrators barred doors and locked gates to keep them inside. Helmeted police arrived at the campuses, arrested some students, and escorted others to the principal’s office, and at least two beatings by officers were reported at Roosevelt. Parents and neighbors joined the protest, and it spread to other schools. Over the following days, estimates of the number of students who took part ranged from about fifteen thousand to more than twenty thousand, from seven high schools.

The students and their allies were not asking for chaos. Through a committee of students, parents, teachers, and community members, they drew up a list of demands, 39 in all by one account. They called for bilingual education, courses in Mexican American history and culture, more Latino teachers and administrators, expanded school libraries, and an end to the discrimination they faced in their classrooms. On March 11, the committee tried to present the demands to the Los Angeles Board of Education. On March 28, the board finally discussed them, and then concluded that it lacked the money to make the changes.

The authorities answered the protest with arrests. On March 31, police arrested Sal Castro and twelve other organizers, who became known as the “East L.A. 13.” They were charged with offenses that included disturbing the peace. Castro lost his job at Lincoln, but after protesters staged sit-ins at school board meetings, he was reinstated. The East L.A. 13 were released on bail by June 2, and in 1970 the court dropped all of the charges.

The walkouts did not immediately transform the schools, and the board’s answer showed how far the change still had to go. Yet historians regard the blowouts as a turning point for the Chicano Movement. They carried the energy of the farmworkers’ struggle from the fields of California into the city, and they marked the first major protest led by Chicano youth. Many of the students who walked out in 1968 went on to join the Chicano Moratorium, the antiwar march of August 29, 1970, which became the largest protest in Los Angeles history up to that time. A generation that had been taught that its language and history did not belong in school had decided to demand that they did.

Analytical Questions
  1. Analyze the Experience: What conditions in East Los Angeles high schools led thousands of students to walk out of class in March 1968?
  2. Interpret Motives: Why did Sal Castro first urge students to take their grievances to the school board, and why did the board’s response push them toward a walkout?
  3. Evaluate the Impact: The walkouts did not immediately change school conditions. Do you agree with historians that they were still a turning point for the Chicano Movement? Explain your reasoning.
