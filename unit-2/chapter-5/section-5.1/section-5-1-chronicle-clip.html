American Chronicles: The Hottest Ticket in History

Washington, D.C., May 1868 · from American Chronicles, Chapter 5: The Broken South

On the morning of May 16, 1868, the United States Senate Chamber ceased to be a place of legislation and transformed into a theater of high-stakes drama. The city of Washington was in a frenzy. For weeks, the only topic of conversation in the boarding houses, saloons, and hotel lobbies had been the “Great Trial.” The President of the United States, Andrew Johnson, stood accused of “high crimes and misdemeanors,” specifically for violating the Tenure of Office Act by firing his Secretary of War, Edwin Stanton.

The demand to witness the proceedings was unlike anything the capital had ever seen. The Senate gallery, which usually held a few bored journalists and tourists, was suddenly the most exclusive destination in America. Only one thousand tickets were printed for each day of the trial. These small slips of cardstock, color-coded by day (green for Monday, pink for Tuesday, and so on), became more valuable than gold. Scalpers roamed Pennsylvania Avenue, selling tickets that were ostensibly free for as much as $50—a month’s wages for a skilled laborer.

Inside the chamber, the atmosphere was electric and suffocating. The gallery was packed to capacity, largely with the wives and daughters of the political elite, dressed in their finest silks and holding opera glasses to get a better view of the “prisoner” (though Johnson, on the advice of his lawyers, wisely stayed away from the Capitol). On the floor below, the Senators sat at their desks, acting as the jury. Chief Justice Salmon P. Chase, in his flowing judicial robes, presided from the Vice President’s chair. The air was thick with heat, perfume, and tension.

The trial had dragged on for two months, a tedious parade of legal arguments about the definition of “removal” and “conspiracy.” But by mid-May, the legal hair-splitting was over. It all came down to the math. The Constitution required a two-thirds majority to convict. There were 54 senators. The Radical Republicans needed 36 votes to remove Johnson. They counted 35 “guilty” votes as certain. They needed just one more.

All eyes turned to the “undecideds,” a small group of moderate Republicans who were visibly agonizing over their decision. The pressure on them was immense. They received death threats in the mail and visits from powerful party bosses who warned them that a vote for acquittal would be political suicide. The spotlight zeroed in on Edmund G. Ross, a quiet, freshman senator from Kansas. Ross was a radical abolitionist who had fought in the Union army; surely, he would vote to remove the “traitor” Johnson. But Ross remained silent, refusing to tell anyone how he would vote.

As the clerk began to call the roll for the vote on the eleventh article of impeachment, the chamber fell deathly silent. “Senator Anthony?” “Guilty.” “Senator Bayard?” “Not Guilty.” The list went on, alphabetical and agonizingly slow. When the clerk reached “Senator Ross,” people in the gallery leaned over the railings, holding their breath. Ross stood up at his desk. He looked pale and physically shaken. He later wrote that he felt as if he were looking into his own open grave. “Mr. Senator Ross,” the Chief Justice asked, “how say you?” Ross’s voice was quiet but clear: “Not Guilty.”

A gasp rippled through the room. The Radicals had lost. Ross’s vote broke the momentum. When the final tally was announced—35 guilty, 19 not guilty—Johnson had survived by a single vote. The “hottest ticket” in Washington had delivered a finale that stunned the nation. The presidency was saved, but the dream of a unified Republican party was shattered. Ross was true to the threats he received; he was vilified in the press, accused of bribery, and never held political office again. He had saved the constitutional balance of power, but he had paid for the ticket with his career.
