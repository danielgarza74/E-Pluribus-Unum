SECTION6.1

Steel, Steam, and the Closing of the Range

Introduction

Corporate Conquest

In the decades following the Civil War, the American West was transformed from a perceived “wild” frontier into a calculated industrial and government-sponsored project. Far from the myth of rugged individualism, the incorporation of the West was driven by a powerful partnership between the federal government and massive corporations seeking to extract the continent’s immense resources. Through the expansion of the steam locomotive and landmark legislation like the Homestead Act, the nation’s identity and economy were fundamentally altered as the Pacific coast was bound to the industrial North. This era saw the rise of global agricultural markets, the birth of the Cattle Kingdom, and the eventual enclosure of the plains by barbed wire.

The initial conquest of the West required an infrastructure project of unprecedented scale: the Transcontinental Railroad. Because the financial risk was too great for private investors alone, the federal government became an indispensable partner, providing massive land grants in a “checkerboard” pattern to subsidize construction. Key Concepts

The Engine of Consolidation

In the decades following the Civil War, the American West underwent a transformation so rapid and profound that it fundamentally altered the nation’s identity and economy. Often depicted in mythology as a triumph of rugged individualism—where solitary pioneers tamed a wild continent with nothing but grit and a rifle—the reality of western expansion was far more organized, industrial, and government-sponsored. The incorporation of the West was not a haphazard migration but a calculated project of the federal government, driven by the desire to bind the Pacific coast to the industrial North and to extract the immense resources of the continent’s interior. The primary instrument of this transformation was the steam locomotive.

Before the 1860s, travel to the West was a perilous months-long journey by wagon or a long sea voyage around South America. The solution to this geographic isolation lay in the construction of the Transcontinental Railroad, a feat of engineering that would bridge the gap between the Missouri River and the Pacific Ocean. However, the sheer scale of such a project was beyond the financial risk appetite of private investors. Building thousands of miles of track through the Great Plains, over the Rockies, and across the scorched Great Basin required capital that no single corporation possessed. To solve this, the federal government stepped in, becoming the indispensable partner of industry.

Congress passed the Pacific Railway Acts of 1862 and 1864, which chartered two companies to undertake the task: the Union Pacific and the Central Pacific. The Union Pacific was to build westward from Omaha, Nebraska, while the Central Pacific would build eastward from Sacramento, California. To finance this colossal endeavor, the government did not simply hand over cash; it handed over the continent itself. The government granted these companies massive loans and, more importantly, vast tracts of public land. For every mile of track laid, the railroad companies received ten square miles of land in alternating sections on either side of the track—a pattern that looked like a checkerboard.

This land grant system had profound consequences. It made the railroad companies not just transportation firms, but the largest real estate developers in the nation. They financed construction by selling these government-gifted lands to settlers, speculators, and European investors. The railroads sent agents to Scandinavia, Germany, and Great Britain to recruit immigrants with promises of fertile soil and cheap passage, effectively populating the West to create their own customer base. The federal subsidy system ensured that the settlement of the West was tied inextricably to corporate profit. The railroads determined where towns would be built, which farmers would succeed based on freight rates, and which regions would stagnate.

The construction of the line was a race, as each mile of track meant more land and more government bonds. This competition drove a brutal pace of labor. The Union Pacific relied heavily on Irish immigrants and Civil War veterans, who laid track across the flat expanses of the Plains while fending off attacks from Native American tribes who saw the “iron horse” as a death knell for their way of life. The Central Pacific, facing the granite wall of the Sierra Nevada, turned to Chinese laborers. These workers blasted tunnels through solid rock and worked through avalanches and blizzards, suffering high casualty rates that were often glossed over in company reports. The two lines finally met on May 10, 1869, at Promontory Point, Utah. A golden spike was driven into the final tie, sending a telegraph signal to the East Coast that set off celebrations of national unity. The continent had been shrunk; a journey that once took months now took days.

The impact of the railroads extended beyond mere travel time. To manage the scheduling of trains moving across the vast breadth of the continent, the railroad companies unilaterally divided the nation into four Standard Time Zones in 1883. Prior to this, every town kept its own local sun time, a nightmare for schedulers. The fact that the federal government later adopted these railroad time zones into law illustrates the immense power these corporations held: they literally redefined time for the American people.

Key Concepts

The Agrarian Dream and Reality

While the railroads laid the infrastructure for the West, the federal government also sought to populate the land with loyal citizens. The Republican party, dominating Congress during the Civil War, passed the Homestead Act in 1862. This landmark legislation reflected the Jeffersonian ideal of a nation of small, independent farmers. It offered 160 acres of public land free to any citizen, or intended citizen, who was head of a household. The only requirements were to pay a small registration fee, live on the land for five years, and “improve” it by building a dwelling and cultivating crops.

On paper, the Homestead Act was a democratic triumph, offering the poor a chance at property ownership that was impossible in Europe or the crowded East. Between 1862 and 1900, up to 600,000 families took advantage of this offer. However, the reality of the Homestead Act was often far harsher than the dream. The law was written by legislators from the lush, wet woodlands of the East who did not understand the geography of the West. West of the 100th meridian, the climate is semi-arid, receiving less than 20 inches of rain a year. In these dry regions, 160 acres was often too large to irrigate by hand but too small to practice the dry-farming techniques necessary to yield a profit.

Settlers on the Great Plains, known as Sodbusters, faced a grueling existence. Lacking trees for timber, they built their homes out of stacked blocks of prairie turf. These sod houses were cool in the summer and warm in the winter, but they were dark, damp, and prone to infestation by snakes and insects. The environment itself seemed hostile. Farmers battled blizzards, tornadoes, and terrifying plagues of locusts that could devour a year’s worth of crops in hours. Water was a constant obsession; deep wells had to be drilled, often by hand, to reach the water table.

The economic reality of the frontier also challenged the ideal of the independent yeoman farmer. Successful farming on the Plains increasingly required expensive machinery—steel plows to break the tough root systems of the prairie grass, mechanical reapers to harvest wheat, and grain drills to plant seeds deep where moisture remained. Most homesteaders lacked the capital to buy this equipment, forcing them to take out loans at high interest rates. Consequently, while the Homestead Act helped many, a significant portion of the land ended up in the hands of speculators and large corporations. “Bonanza farms”—enormous single-crop operations of 15,000 to 50,000 acres—began to appear. These corporate farms operated like factories, using economies of scale and professional management to outproduce and undersell the small family farmer.

Despite these hardships, the pull of the land remained strong, particularly for African Americans in the South following the collapse of Reconstruction. In 1879, a mass migration known as the “Exodus” occurred, as thousands of African Americans moved from the post-Reconstruction South to Kansas. Known as Exodusters, these migrants sought to escape the violence of the Ku Klux Klan and the economic slavery of sharecropping. They saw the West as a promised land of freedom. While some Exodusters managed to establish successful communities, many found that the best land had already been taken by railroad companies or speculators, and they often faced the same poverty on the plains that they had fled in the South, though they remained fiercely proud of their independence from white oversight.

To support this agricultural expansion, the federal government passed the Morrill Land Grant Act of 1862 and 1890. This act gave federal land to the states to help finance agricultural colleges. These institutions were tasked with developing new farming techniques and machinery adapted to the unique conditions of the American West. The research produced by these “land-grant colleges” eventually helped turn the arid plains into the nation’s breadbasket, but often by promoting the very industrial farming methods that favored large operations over the small homesteader.

SECTION 6.1 · PART 2The Enclosed Horizon

Before the Plains were fully subdivided by farmers, the region was dominated by the Cattle Kingdom, an industry built on Spanish and Mexican traditions and the high demand for beef in Northern cities. Cowboys, including a diverse workforce of Mexican Vaqueros and African Americans, drove millions of cattle along trails to railroad hubs in an era defined by the “Long Drive”. This iconic period of the open range lasted only twenty years before it was dismantled by overgrazing, harsh winters, and the invention of barbed wire. As the frontier closed and the West became a grid of private property, marginalized farmers began organizing into the Grange to challenge the corporate monopolies that now controlled their economic destiny.

Key Concepts

The Rise of the Cattle Kingdom

Before the farmers fully fenced in the Plains, the vast grasslands from Texas to Montana were the domain of the cattle rancher. The roots of this industry lay in Texas, where Spanish and Mexican traditions had established the techniques of open-range ranching long before American settlers arrived. The Texas Longhorn, a breed descended from Spanish cattle, was perfectly adapted to the harsh environment; it could survive on coarse grass and little water and was immune to widespread diseases like Texas fever. By the end of the Civil War, millions of these cattle roamed wild in Texas.

QUICK REFERENCELonghorn A hardy breed descended from Spanish cattle that could survive on coarse grass and little water. Spanish: ganado longhorn
CONNECT TO ECONOMICSA steer that was worth a few dollars in Texas could bring forty or fifty dollars in the North. That gap between supply and demand created the Cattle Kingdom.

The economic driver of the cattle boom was the disconnection between supply and demand. In Texas, a steer might be worth only a few dollars. In the rapidly industrializing cities of the North, where hungry workers demanded beef, that same steer could command forty or fifty dollars. The challenge was logistics: how to move the beef from the range to the railhead. This economic opportunity gave rise to the Cattle Kingdom and the iconic era of the Long Drive.

Ranchers hired cowboys to herd cattle hundreds of miles north from Texas to the nearest railroad towns in Kansas, Missouri, and Nebraska. Routes like the Chisholm Trail became arteries of commerce, funneling millions of cattle to towns like Abilene and Dodge City. These “cow towns” became famous for their rowdiness, but their primary function was industrial: they were loading docks where living resources were transferred to the Union Pacific or Kansas Pacific tracks for shipment to the slaughterhouses of Chicago.

The labor force that drove this industry was far more diverse than Hollywood westerns typically depict. The very tools and techniques of the American cowboy—the lasso, the saddle, the chaps, and even the word “rodeo”—were borrowed directly from the Mexican Vaqueros. Furthermore, it is estimated that one in four cowboys on the long drives was African American, and another sizable percentage was Mexican. For many young men, the cattle drive offered a paying job and a life of relative equality on the trail, where skill with a horse and a rope mattered more than skin color. However, it was grueling, dangerous work, characterized by long hours in the saddle, the threat of stampedes, and constant exposure to the elements, all for low wages.

Key Concepts

Closing the Frontier

The era of the open range and the long drive was brief, lasting only about twenty years. Its demise was brought about by a combination of environmental limits and technological innovation. As homesteaders and sodbusters moved onto the Plains in greater numbers, conflict arose between the ranchers, who needed open land for grazing, and farmers, who needed to protect their crops from trampling herds. In a land with few trees, building wooden fences was prohibitively expensive.

The solution came in 1874, when an Illinois farmer named Joseph Glidden invented a practical, mass-producible form of Barbed Wire. This simple invention changed the geography of the West forever. Cheap and effective, barbed wire allowed farmers to fence off their claims, physically blocking the cattle trails that had sustained the long drives. At the same time, forward-thinking ranchers began to realize that the open range model was unsustainable. Overgrazing had degraded the grasslands, and severe weather events—specifically the harsh winters of 1886-1887—wiped out enormous herds of cattle that had been left to fend for themselves on the open plains.

Ranching transformed from a nomadic adventure into a settled business. Ranchers began to buy land, fence it with barbed wire, and raise hay to feed their stock during the winter. They imported blooded stock to improve the quality of their beef, moving away from the hardy but stringy Longhorn. The Cattle Kingdom did not disappear, but it was fenced in and corporatized. The romantic figure of the cowboy became a ranch hand, a wage laborer who spent as much time repairing fences as he did riding horses.

This transition marked the effective closure of the frontier. The West was no longer an open expanse but a grid of private property, connected by railroads and divided by wire. The integration of the West into the national economy was complete. Farmers, now tied to the global market for their wheat and corn, found themselves at the mercy of railroad freight rates and grain elevator monopolies. Feeling squeezed by these corporate powers, they began to organize. In 1867, Oliver Hudson Kelley founded the Patrons of Husbandry, commonly known as The Grange. Originally a social outlet for isolated farm families, the Grange quickly evolved into a political force, fighting for government regulation of railroad rates and grain storage fees. This agrarian unrest would eventually blossom into the Populist movement, challenging the very corporate-government partnership that had opened the West in the first place.

The Checkerboard Land Grant System

Location and Landscape

The Pacific Railway Acts of 1862 and 1864 granted the Union Pacific and Central Pacific railroads vast swathes of public land to subsidize construction. This was not a solid block of land, but a “checkerboard” pattern extending 10 to 20 miles on either side of the track.

Geography and Events

The government retained every other square-mile section (the white squares), while the railroads received the alternating sections (the black squares). This meant that for every mile of track laid, the railroad received ten sections (6,400 acres) of land. The logic was that the railroad would increase the value of the government’s retained land, which could then be sold at a higher price.

Regional Impact

This grant system made the railroads the arbiters of western geography. They decided where towns would be built and promoted specific crops to ensure freight business. However, this also led to corruption, as railroads often claimed the best land (with water access) and sold it for high profits, while the “public” land available to homesteaders was often further from the tracks or less desirable.

Broader Implications

The checkerboard pattern permanently shaped land ownership in the West. It created a patchwork of private corporate land and public domain that complicates land management to this day. It effectively privatized a massive portion of the national commons to finance industrial development.

The Wars for the Plains

Introduction

The Vanishing Frontier

As the smoke cleared from the Civil War, the United States turned its full attention toward the vast grasslands of the interior, a region once dismissed as uninhabitable. This area, home to powerful nomadic nations, became the stage for a brutal struggle over the meaning of land and the future of indigenous sovereignty. The federal government utilized both devastating military strategies and coercive legislative tools to dismantle the tribal way of life. This chapter explores the tragic sequence of events that transformed the Great Plains from an open range into a landscape of confinement and forced cultural change.

SECTION 6.2 · PART 1The Broken Prairie

The initial encounter between the expanding United States and the Plains tribes was defined by a fundamental disagreement over land use—one side saw a commodity to be fenced, while the other saw a sacred open range. As railroads and settlers encroached, the federal government moved from loose oversight to the restrictive Reservation System, intended to clear the path for white civilization. When tribes resisted this confinement, U.S. military leaders applied the ruthless logic of “total war,” recognizing that they could not defeat a mobile enemy without first destroying the biological foundation of their existence: the buffalo

Key Concepts

The Collision of Two Worlds

The geographic expanse known as the Great Plains stretches from the Dakotas south to Texas and from the Mississippi River west to the Rocky Mountains. For decades, American mapmakers had labeled this region the “Great American Desert,” deeming it unfit for white civilization due to its lack of timber and reliable rainfall. This dismissal, however, served as a protective barrier for the indigenous nations that called the grasslands home. By the mid-19th century, the Plains were dominated by powerful, horse-mounted tribes including the Sioux (Lakota), Cheyenne, Arapaho, Comanche, and Kiowa. These nations had developed a highly mobile, warrior culture that was perfectly adapted to the semi-arid environment. They were not monolithic; while some groups practiced agriculture, the defining feature of the Plains culture was the chase.

Central to this way of life was the Buffalo (Bison). This massive animal was the biological foundation of the Plains economy. A single buffalo provided hundreds of pounds of meat for sustenance, hides for tepees and winter robes, sinew for bowstrings, and bones for tools. The migration patterns of the buffalo herds dictated the rhythm of life, requiring the tribes to remain nomadic. This mobility was their greatest strategic asset, but it was also the source of their inevitable conflict with the United States. To the American government and the settlers moving west, land was a commodity to be measured, fenced, and owned. To the Plains tribes, the land was an open range to be traversed.

As the Civil War concluded, the pressure on the Plains intensified. The construction of the Transcontinental Railroad sliced through the heart of tribal hunting grounds. Miners flooded into Colorado and Montana, ignoring treaty boundaries in their search for gold. In response, the federal government adopted a more aggressive approach to the “Indian Question.” The loose oversight of the past was replaced by the Reservation System, a policy designed to concentrate Native American populations onto clearly defined, often restricted tracts of land. The goal was twofold: to clear the vast majority of the land for white settlement and railroads, and to force the nomadic tribes to adopt a sedentary, agricultural lifestyle. The tribes, however, refused to submit quietly to this confinement, leading to a series of brutal conflicts that would engulf the West for nearly three decades.

Key Concepts

Flashpoints of Violence

The cycle of violence that defined the post-Civil War West did not begin with a single declaration of war but with a series of tragic misunderstandings and brutal massacres that eroded trust on both sides. One of the most infamous incidents occurred in Colorado in 1864. As tensions rose between miners and the Cheyenne and Arapaho tribes, a peace chief named Black Kettle sought protection for his band. Assured of safety by U.S. military officials, he camped along Sand Creek flying an American flag and a white truce flag above his lodge.

On the morning of November 29, 1864, Colonel John Chivington led a militia force of 700 men against the sleeping village. Ignoring the flags and the fact that most of the camp’s warriors were out hunting, Chivington ordered his troops to “Kill and scalp all, big and little; nits make lice.” The resulting Sand Creek Massacre was horrific. Over 150 Cheyenne and Arapaho, mostly women, children, and the elderly, were slaughtered and mutilated. The brutality of Sand Creek shocked Eastern reformers but radicalized the Plains tribes. It proved to many young warriors that the white man’s treaties were worthless and his promises fatal.

Resistance intensified across the Plains. In the north, the Lakota Sioux, led by Red Cloud, fought a successful campaign to close the Bozeman Trail, a route to the Montana gold fields that cut through their prime hunting grounds. In 1866, warriors lured an overconfident U.S. officer, Captain William Fetterman, into an ambush, killing him and all 80 of his men. The government, realizing it could not militarily defeat the united tribes at that time, sued for peace. The resulting Treaty of Fort Laramie (1868) was a temporary victory for the Sioux, abandoning the Bozeman Trail and guaranteeing the “Great Sioux Reservation,” including the sacred Black Hills, to the tribes in perpetuity. However, this peace was fragile, built on the government’s hope that the reservation system would eventually pacify the tribes without further bloodshed—a hope that would soon be dashed by the discovery of gold.

Key Concepts

The Strategy of Total War

The U.S. Army, tasked with enforcing the reservation policy, faced a difficult enemy. The Plains warriors were expert horsemen and guerrilla fighters who knew the terrain intimately. They could strike isolated wagon trains or settlements and then vanish into the vastness of the prairie before heavy columns of infantry or cavalry could respond. Conventional European tactics of line battles and sieges were useless against such a mobile force. Frustrated by their inability to bring the tribes to a decisive engagement, Union Army veterans like Generals William Tecumseh Sherman and Philip Sheridan applied the brutal lessons they had learned during the Civil War: the concept of “total war.”

Total war dictates that to defeat an enemy, one must destroy not just their army, but the economic and psychological resources that sustain their ability to fight. On the Plains, this meant attacking the tribes during the winter when they were most vulnerable and, most devastatingly, targeting the buffalo. General Sheridan correctly identified the buffalo as the “commissary” of the Plains tribes. As long as the herds roamed free, the tribes could remain independent of the government. If the buffalo were gone, the tribes would face a stark choice: surrender to the reservation and accept government food rations, or face starvation.

The destruction of the buffalo was industrial in scale and speed. The railroads facilitated this slaughter by bringing professional hunters and “sportsmen” deep into the herds’ territory. Hunters killed the animals by the thousands, stripping the hides for leather belts in eastern factories and leaving the carcasses to rot on the prairie. It is estimated that between 1865 and 1875, the buffalo population collapsed from over 15 million to fewer than a thousand. This was not merely hunting; it was ecological genocide engineered for political ends. The U.S. Army provided ammunition to hunters and protected them, viewing them as more effective than soldiers in pacifying the tribes.

The impact on the Plains Indians was catastrophic. The disappearance of the buffalo destroyed their economy, their shelter, and their spiritual practices. Warriors who had once roamed thousands of miles were reduced to waiting in lines at reservation agencies for handouts of beef and flour, which were often spoiled or stolen by corrupt Indian Agents. This enforced dependency broke the power of the tribal chiefs and shattered the social cohesion of the nations. The “taming” of the West was thus achieved as much by the Sharps buffalo rifle as by the military campaigns themselves.

SECTION 6.2 · PART 2Subjugation and Cultural Transformation

While military conflict reached a fever pitch on the northern plains with rare tribal victories, the overwhelming industrial might of the United States eventually forced a surrender. However, the end of armed hostiles did not mean the end of the conflict; it merely shifted from the battlefield to the classroom and the surveyor’s map. Through policies of Assimilation, reformers and legislators sought to “civilize” Native Americans by dismantling communal tribal identities in favor of private property and forced education. This era of cultural reconstruction culminated in the heartbreaking violence at Wounded Knee, effectively closing the frontier to indigenous independence.

Key Concepts

The Crisis on the Northern Plains

While the southern tribes (like the Comanche and Kiowa) were subjugated by the mid-1870s through the Red River War, resistance remained fierce on the Northern Plains. The Sioux (Lakota), led by determined chiefs and spiritual leaders, posed the most significant military challenge to U.S. expansion. The conflict came to a head in the Black Hills of South Dakota. The Treaty of Fort Laramie (1868) had guaranteed the Black Hills to the Sioux as part of their reservation “as long as the grass shall grow.” However, in 1874, an expedition led by Lieutenant Colonel George Custer confirmed the presence of gold in the hills.

The discovery triggered a gold rush. Thousands of white prospectors illegally flooded into the Sioux reservation. The federal government, rather than enforcing the treaty and removing the miners, tried to buy the Black Hills from the Sioux. The tribes refused to sell their sacred land. In response, the government declared that all Sioux who did not report to their agencies by January 31, 1876, would be considered “hostile.” It was an impossible deadline to meet in the dead of winter, and many bands, led by the charismatic Sitting Bull and the brilliant tactical commander Crazy Horse, had no intention of complying.

In the summer of 1876, the U.S. Army launched a massive three-pronged offensive to round up these “hostiles.” The campaign culminated in one of the most famous and mythologized events in American history. On June 25, 1876, Custer’s 7th Cavalry located a massive encampment of Sioux and Cheyenne along the Little Bighorn River in Montana Territory. Custer, seeking glory and vastly underestimating the size of the village, divided his forces and attacked without waiting for reinforcements.

The Battle of Little Bighorn was a stunning victory for the Native American alliance. The encampment contained thousands of warriors who, inspired by Sitting Bull’s vision of soldiers falling into camp “like grasshoppers,” fought with desperate ferocity. Custer and all 260 men of his immediate command were surrounded and killed in less than an hour. The victory, however, was the beginning of the end for the Sioux. News of “Custer’s Last Stand” reached the East Coast just as the nation was celebrating its Centennial (100th anniversary). The public was outraged, viewing the battle as a massacre of American heroes.

The U.S. government responded with overwhelming force. Congress authorized thousands more troops to flood the region. Throughout the winter of 1876-1877, the Army relentlessly pursued the scattered bands, preventing them from hunting or resting. Starving and freezing, the Sioux were forced to surrender one by one. Crazy Horse turned himself in and was later killed in a struggle at a guardhouse. Sitting Bull led a band into Canada but eventually returned to surrender in 1881, ending the era of major military resistance on the Northern Plains.

Key Concepts

The Politics of Assimilation

With the tribes confined to reservations, the government’s policy shifted from military conquest to cultural reconstruction. In the East, a growing movement of humanitarian reformers began to critique the government’s history of violence and broken promises. A pivotal figure in this movement was Helen Hunt Jackson, whose 1881 book A Century of Dishonor meticulously documented the systemic fraud and betrayal characterizing U.S. dealings with Native Americans. While Jackson helped awaken the nation’s conscience, the solution proposed by reformers was often just as destructive as the military campaigns.

The reformers believed that the Native American could only survive by vanishing culturally—by becoming “civilized” according to white American standards. This meant abandoning tribal identities, communal living, and native religions in favor of individualism, private property, and Christianity. This philosophy, known as Assimilation, became the official policy of the United States.

The legal instrument for this transformation was the Dawes Severalty Act of 1887. This legislation sought to dismantle the reservation system by breaking up tribal land ownership. Instead of the tribe owning the land collectively, the Dawes Act allotted 160 acres to each head of household and 80 acres to single adults. The government believed that private land ownership would magically transform hunters into self-sufficient farmers. However, the policy was a disaster for Native Americans. The allotted land was often in arid regions unsuitable for small-scale agriculture. Furthermore, the act declared that any reservation land remaining after allotment was “surplus” and could be sold to white settlers.

The result was a massive transfer of wealth. In 1887, tribes held 138 million acres of land. By 1934, when the policy was finally reversed, that amount had shrunk to 48 million acres. The best farmland ended up in white hands, while Native Americans were left with the scrap. The Dawes Act undermined the tribal structure by treating individuals as separate from the group, effectively striking at the heart of indigenous social organization.

Cultural assimilation was also pursued through education. The government removed thousands of Native American children from their families and sent them to boarding schools, the most famous being the Carlisle Indian School in Pennsylvania. Founded by Richard Henry Pratt, a former army officer, the school operated under the motto: “Kill the Indian and save the man.” Students were subjected to a military-style regimen. They had their long hair shorn, were dressed in uniforms, and were severely punished for speaking their native languages or practicing their religions. The curriculum focused on vocational training, preparing the students for lives as manual laborers or domestic servants. While some graduates used their education to fight for tribal rights, for many, the experience was deeply traumatic, creating a generation alienated from both their own heritage and white society.

Key Concepts

The Final Tragedy: Wounded Knee

By 1890, the Sioux on the reservations were in a state of despair. Their land had been taken, their herds destroyed, and their culture outlawed. Disease and starvation were rampant. Into this misery came a message of hope from a Paiute prophet named Wovoka. He preached a new religious vision known as the Ghost Dance Movement. Wovoka promised that if the people lived peacefully and performed the sacred Ghost Dance, the white man would disappear, the buffalo would return, and their ancestors would rise from the dead to inhabit a renewed earth.

The Ghost Dance was a non-violent religious response to cultural collapse, but to the nervous white agents and settlers surrounding the reservations, it looked like the preparation for an uprising. The rhythmic dancing and the gathering of large crowds terrified officials, who called for military support. The government decided to arrest the tribal leaders they believed were fomenting this “rebellion,” specifically targeting Sitting Bull at the Standing Rock Agency. On December 15, 1890, tribal police sent to arrest Sitting Bull killed him during a struggle, further inflaming tensions.

Fearful of reprisal, a band of about 350 Miniconjou Sioux under Chief Big Foot fled, seeking safety at the Pine Ridge Agency. They were intercepted by the 7th Cavalry—Custer’s old regiment—and brought to a campsite at Wounded Knee Creek. On the morning of December 29, 1890, the soldiers began to disarm the freezing and starving Sioux. During the tension of the search, a gun was discharged. The soldiers, positioned on the ridges above the camp with Hotchkiss machine guns, immediately opened fire on the crowd.

The Wounded Knee Massacre was not a battle; it was a slaughter. Within minutes, roughly 300 Native Americans lay dead or dying, including women and children who were chased down and shot as they fled. The soldiers left the bodies to freeze in a blizzard that swept in that afternoon. The photographs of the frozen corpses in the snow remain some of the most haunting images in American history. Wounded Knee marked the symbolic and bloody end of the Indian Wars. The armed resistance of the Plains tribes had been crushed, and the reservation system was firmly established. The frontier, as a place of indigenous sovereignty, was closed.

The Fractured Frontier

Introduction

The Collision of Cultures

The late nineteenth-century American West is often remembered as a lonely expanse of untamed wilderness, but the historical reality was a vibrant and often violent collision of diverse peoples. As the United States pushed its borders to the Pacific, the region functioned less like a simple line of settlement and more like a complex meeting ground for Native Americans, Hispanic communities, and waves of Asian and European immigrants. This expansion was fueled by a multiracial workforce that transformed the landscape, even as a growing white majority sought to impose a rigid racial and legal hierarchy over the land. The struggle for belonging in this era would redefine the American legal system and create lasting tensions over the meaning of citizenship and property.

SECTION 6.3 · PART 1The Multiracial Borderland

The development of the West was not the work of a single group of pioneers, but rather the result of a massive, multiracial labor force. While popular mythology often centered on the white male settler, the physical infrastructure and economic foundation of the region were built by a diverse array of hands. This section explores the meeting of these cultures and the essential roles they played in integrating the West into the American economy.

QUICK REFERENCEMultiracial Made up of people of many races and ethnic groups. Spanish: multirracial
FAST FACT3 groupsThe chapter points to Chinese, Mexican, and African American laborers as key builders of the West.

Key Concepts

The Myth and the Reality

In the American imagination, the West has long been portrayed as a blank canvas—a rugged wilderness where white pioneers, through grit and individualism, built a civilization from scratch. This narrative was popularized in dime novels and later in Hollywood films, constructing a “frontier” that was presented as overwhelmingly white and male. These stories focused on the lone pioneer taming the desert, ignoring the fact that the region was already a home to established civilizations. By framing the West as “empty,” these myths justified the displacement of those who had lived there for centuries.

However, the historical reality of the trans-Mississippi West was radically different. It was not merely a frontier of settlement but a complex Borderlands of encounter, conflict, and exchange among diverse peoples. Long before the arrival of Anglo-American settlers, the region was populated by indigenous nations and Hispanic communities with deep roots in the soil. As the United States expanded, this mix was further enriched by waves of Asian immigrants crossing the Pacific and African Americans moving west from the South to escape the constraints of the post-Reconstruction era.

The integration of the West into the national economy was driven by this multiracial workforce, rather than the efforts of a single group. The tunnels of the Sierra Nevada were blasted by Chinese crews; the cattle of the Great Plains were herded by Mexican, Black, and white laborers; and the agricultural bounty of California was harvested by a kaleidoscope of people from across the globe. One of the most enduring symbols of the erasure of this diversity occurred on May 10, 1869, at Promontory Point, Utah. When the ceremonial Golden Spike was driven to connect the Central Pacific and Union Pacific railroads, the official photographs conspicuously excluded the thousands of Chinese laborers who had performed the most dangerous work on the line.

Key Concepts

The Cowboy and the Borderland

The iconic figure of the American West, the Cowboy, was itself a product of this complex borderland history rather than a purely Anglo-American invention. The cattle industry of the Great Plains was directly adapted from the Mexican ranching system that had thrived in the Southwest for centuries. The equipment used by cattlemen—including the lariat, the specialized saddle, and leather chaps—was Mexican in origin. Even the vocabulary of the trail, from “rodeo” and “corral” to “bronco,” was borrowed from the Spanish language, reflecting the deep cultural roots of the industry.

Furthermore, the workforce on the legendary cattle trails was deeply multiracial. Historians estimate that at least one-third of the cowboys on the long drives were Mexican or African American. For many Black men, the West offered a degree of autonomy and social equality that was impossible in the Jim Crow South. On the trail, a man’s status was often determined by his skill with a horse or a rope rather than the color of his skin. While they still faced discrimination in the “cow towns” at the end of the drive, the trail itself functioned as a unique space of professional integration.

Despite this reality, the “whitewashing” of the West in popular culture later replaced these Black and Brown laborers with the image of the white gunslinger. By the early 20th century, the diverse origins of the ranching industry were largely scrubbed from the national memory to fit a more homogeneous vision of American identity. This process of cultural erasure mirrored the political and economic dispossession occurring across the region, where the contributions of non-white workers were utilized for profit while their rights and histories were systematically suppressed.

Key Concepts

The “Chinese Question” and the Politics of Exclusion

The Chinese presence in the West began in earnest with the California Gold Rush, but the construction of the Transcontinental Railroad brought even greater numbers of young men from the Pearl River Delta. Recruited by the Central Pacific Railroad for work that many white laborers refused, Chinese workers proved indispensable. They exhibited immense skill and courage, lowering themselves in baskets down sheer cliffs to plant explosives and enduring the brutal winters of the high Sierras. Despite these contributions, they were often denied the recognition and safety afforded to their white counterparts.

As the economy dipped in the 1870s, white workers began to view the Chinese as convenient scapegoats for their own financial struggles. This sentiment was crystalized in the rise of Nativism—a political policy of promoting the interests of native-born inhabitants against those of immigrants. In California, this took a specifically anti-Asian form. The political face of this movement was Dennis Kearney, an Irish immigrant and leader of the Workingmen’s Party of California. Kearney was a demagogue who whipped up crowds in San Francisco with fiery speeches blaming “railroad magnates” and the Chinese for the plight of the working class, famously ending his rallies with the slogan, “The Chinese must go!”

The resulting political pressure led to the passage of the Chinese Exclusion Act in 1882. This landmark legislation was the first law in American history to ban a specific group of immigrants solely on the basis of race and class. To enforce the law, the government established a bureaucracy of control at Angel Island in San Francisco Bay, where Chinese arrivals were detained for months. Despite this hostility, the Chinese community fought back. They retreated into urban enclaves known as Chinatowns for safety and used the court system to challenge discrimination. In the 1886 case Yick Wo v. Hopkins, the Supreme Court established the crucial principle that the 14th Amendment’s Equal Protection Clause applied to all persons on U.S. soil, regardless of their citizenship status.

Dispossession in the Southwest

While the Chinese fought for the right to remain, Mexican Americans struggled to hold onto land they had possessed for generations. When the United States acquired the Southwest after the Mexican-American War, the Treaty of Guadalupe Hidalgo (1848) formally guaranteed that Mexican citizens in the ceded territories would enjoy the full rights of U.S. citizens and that their property would be “inviolably respected.” However, as Anglo-American settlers flooded the region, these promises were routinely ignored by both the government and the courts.

The elite landowning class of California, known as the Californios, faced an onslaught of legal and economic challenges. American squatters often refused to recognize the validity of Spanish and Mexican land grants. To settle these disputes, Congress passed the Land Act of 1851, which placed the burden of proof on the landowners. To defend their titles, families had to hire expensive lawyers and navigate a foreign legal system in cases that often lasted decades. Resistance to this dispossession took many forms, ranging from legal challenges to direct action. In New Mexico, a secret organization of night riders known as Las Gorras Blancas (The White Caps) emerged to protect communal grazing lands from the encroachment of Anglo ranchers and railroads. They masked their identities and rode at night to cut fences and burn railroad bridges, acting as a community defense force for the “helpless classes.” Their manifesto made it clear that they were not merely bandits, but protectors of a traditional way of life against corporate and Anglo land grabbers who sought to fence off the common range.

SECTION 6.3 · PART 3The Closing of the Frontier?

By the end of the nineteenth century, the physical expansion of the United States reached a point where the government believed the era of “free land” had ended. This conclusion led to a major re-evaluation of American identity and history, as scholars attempted to explain what the western experience had meant for the nation. This section examines the official declaration of the frontier’s end and the famous historical thesis that attempted to define the American character through the lens of the West.

QUICK REFERENCEFrontier The edge of settled land. In this chapter, also a story Americans told about themselves. Spanish: frontera

Key Concepts

The Census of 1890

By 1890, the Superintendent of the Census issued a startling declaration: a distinct frontier line could no longer be drawn on the American map. For over a century, the census had tracked a clear line where settlement ended and “unsettled” land began. However, by 1890, isolated pockets of settlement had grown and merged to such a degree that the map was now a patchwork of development. The “unsettled” spaces had been pierced by railroads, claimed by massive mining operations, and enclosed by the barbed wire of corporate ranching interests.

This announcement carried profound psychological weight for the American public. The idea that there was always “more room” to the west had been a safety valve for social and economic pressures in the East. If the frontier was gone, many wondered if the opportunity for individual advancement and the uniquely American sense of freedom might also be at risk. The bureaucratic statement from the census office served as a signal that the era of conquest and expansion was transitioning into an era of industrialization and urban growth.

However, the declaration that the West was “settled” was itself a matter of perspective. It ignored the fact that millions of indigenous people and Hispanic residents still lived in these “settled” areas, often under conditions of extreme duress. The closing of the frontier did not mean that the struggles for the region had ended; rather, it meant that the federal government had successfully imposed its administrative and economic systems across the continent. The frontier was not “closed” in a physical sense, but it was being brought under the permanent control of the American state.

Key Concepts

Turner’s Frontier Thesis

The 1890 census announcement inspired a young historian named Frederick Jackson Turner to deliver a paper at the 1893 World’s Columbian Exposition in Chicago. Titled “The Significance of the Frontier in American History,” his work articulated what became known as the Frontier Thesis. Turner argued that the existence of an area of free land and the advance of American settlement westward were the central factors in American development. He claimed that the frontier was a “crucible” where the European immigrant was stripped of old-world habits and forged into a new, distinctly American character.

According to Turner, the frontier experience promoted individualism, democracy, and a practical, inventive mindset. He believed that the constant need to rebuild society on the edge of the wilderness forced Americans to rely on themselves and fostered a spirit of egalitarianism. For decades, this thesis became the dominant interpretation of American history, taught in schools and embraced by politicians as the definitive explanation for why the United States was different from the nations of Europe.

Despite its popularity, modern historians have largely rejected the Frontier Thesis because of its narrow and exclusionary focus. Turner’s vision of the West was an empty stage waiting for white actors, and he completely ignored the complex reality of the region as a place of multiracial conflict. He marginalized Native Americans as part of the “wilderness” to be tamed rather than as people with their own history. Today, the West is viewed not as a closing frontier, but as a permanent zone of Borderlands where the struggles over immigration, land rights, and identity that began in the 19th century continue to define the American experiment.

The Issue

In the mid-19th century, California was the epicenter of a fierce debate over immigration, labor, and national identity. As thousands of Chinese immigrants arrived to work in the gold fields and on railroads, nativist politicians argued they were a threat to white society.

DOCUMENT A · SPEECHA Reformer on Assimilation
“Kill the Indian in him, and save the man.”Richard Henry Pratt, speech on the education of Native Americans, 1892.

Context: Richard Henry Pratt was an army officer who founded the Carlisle Indian School in Pennsylvania in 1879. He gave this speech in 1892, after more than a decade of running the school. Many reformers of his time thought that Native Americans could survive only by giving up their cultures. The line is one of the best-known statements of assimilation.

Analyze:
  1. Close Reading. What does Pratt say should be destroyed, and what does he say should be saved?
  2. Contextualization. According to Section 6.2, what practices at Carlisle put this idea into action, and what did students experience?
  3. Connecting to Chapter 6. How did the Dawes Act and boarding schools work together toward the same goal?
B
DOCUMENT B · LAWA Law Bars Chinese Laborers
“…the coming of Chinese laborers to this country endangers the good order of certain localities…”Chinese Exclusion Act, preamble, approved May 6, 1882.

Context: Congress approved the Chinese Exclusion Act on May 6, 1882. It suspended the immigration of Chinese laborers for ten years, and the ban was later extended. The quotation is from the law’s opening clause, which explains Congress’s reason for passing it.

Analyze:
  1. Close Reading. What reason does the law give for its ban, and whom does it name?
  2. Contextualization. According to Section 6.3, what economic conditions and political movement helped lead to this law?
  3. Connecting to Chapter 6. How does the law’s claim about “good order” compare with what the section says Chinese laborers actually contributed to the West?

DOCUMENT C · HISTORIAN’S ESSAYThe Frontier Has Gone
“The existence of an area of free land, its continuous recession, and the advance of American settlement westward, explain American development.”Frederick Jackson Turner, “The Significance of the Frontier in American History,” 1893.
“…the frontier has gone, and with its going has closed the first period of American history.”Same essay, concluding passage.

Context: Frederick Jackson Turner was a young historian when he read this paper in 1893 at the World’s Columbian Exposition in Chicago. His idea, the Frontier Thesis, shaped how Americans taught their history for decades. Modern historians have criticized it for ignoring the people who already lived in the West.

Analyze:
  1. Close Reading. What does Turner say explains American development?
  2. Contextualization. According to Section 6.3, what announcement from the census inspired Turner, and what groups of people does his picture of the West leave out?
  3. Connecting to Chapter 6. How would the experiences of the Plains nations, Chinese laborers, and Californios change Turner’s story of “free land”?

CHAPTER6

Chapter 6 Review

Essential Question

How was the American West transformed from a diverse borderland into a consolidated part of the national economy, and at what human cost?

Chapter Summary

6.1 Steel, Steam, and the Closing of the Range
The federal government and the railroads built the Transcontinental Railroad with land grants. The Homestead Act promised small farms, but the Plains favored large operations. The Cattle Kingdom rose and then closed as barbed wire, overgrazing, and hard winters fenced in the range.
6.2 The Wars for the Plains
The Army used total war, including the slaughter of the buffalo, to defeat the Plains nations. After Little Bighorn came surrender, the Dawes Act, boarding schools, and the killings at Wounded Knee.
6.3 The Fractured Frontier
The West was a multiracial borderland. Chinese laborers faced nativism and the Chinese Exclusion Act, Californios lost land, and Turner’s Frontier Thesis ignored all of them.
