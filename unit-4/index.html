UNIT 4

The Projection of Power

Chapter 11 opener photo. Image: U.S. Navy / Wikimedia Commons, Public domain

Unit question

Why did the United States abandon its policy of isolation to become a global power, and what were the costs of this new role?

By the end of the nineteenth century the United States had completed its continental expansion and began to look beyond its shores. This unit asks why a nation committed to a policy of isolation became a global power, and what that new role cost.

Chapter 11 follows the shift from continental expansion to overseas empire: the purchase of Alaska and the annexation of Hawaii, the arguments of Frederick Jackson Turner and Alfred Thayer Mahan, the Spanish-American War and the Treaty of Paris, the Open Door in China, and the Panama Canal, the Roosevelt Corollary, and Dollar Diplomacy in the hemisphere. Chapter 12 tests that power in the Great War: neutrality under pressure, mobilization at home, victory abroad, and a peace that failed, including the Treaty of Versailles and the League of Nations.

Students who complete the unit should be able to explain the economic, strategic, and ideological reasons Americans gave for expansion, compare how supporters and opponents of empire argued their cases, and describe how total mobilization reshaped the nation as well as the world.

California standards alignment (unit abstract): 11.4 (Rise to world power), 11.4.1 (Open Door), 11.4.2 (Spanish-American War), 11.4.4 (Roosevelt/Taft/Wilson Diplomacy), 11.4.5 (WWI).
