AMERICAN CHRONICLES · CHAPTER 11 · THE AMERICAN EMPIRE

Trapped in the Forbidden City

The Boxer Siege

← BACK TO CHAPTER 11

In the summer of 1900, the “Open Door” to China was nearly slammed shut by a violent explosion of populist rage. In the dusty streets of Beijing, a secret society known as the Fists of Righteous Harmony---the “Boxers”---roamed the city, fueled by a belief that they were spiritually immune to foreign bullets. Their goal was the total expulsion of the “foreign devils” and the eradication of Christianity from Chinese soil.

By June, the situation had become desperate for the foreign community. Hundreds of diplomats, their families, missionaries, and thousands of Chinese Christian converts fled to the “Legation Quarter,” a small district of foreign embassies located just beneath the walls of the Forbidden City. For eight harrowing weeks, this tiny enclave was cut off from the outside world. The Boxers, supported by elements of the Imperial Chinese Army, launched nightly attacks, raining fire arrows and artillery shells onto the makeshift fortifications.

Inside the legations, the defenders were a ragtag multinational force. American Marines fought alongside British sailors, Japanese soldiers, and Russian guards. Food was strictly rationed---mule meat became a luxury---and water was scarce. The heat of the Beijing summer was oppressive, and the constant threat of being overrun by the “thousands of screaming Boxers” outside the walls created a state of permanent terror. Among the besieged was a young American mining engineer named Herbert Hoover, who would one day become president. He helped organize the defenses and oversaw the distribution of food to the starving refugees.

The siege was finally broken on August 14, 1900, when a multinational rescue force of 20,000 troops, including 2,500 American soldiers, fought their way into the city. The relief of the legations was celebrated in the West as a triumph of civilization over “oriental barbarism.” However, the aftermath was a period of brutal looting and execution by the foreign troops. The Boxer Rebellion proved that the “Open Door” would only stay open if it was held by the barrel of a gun, and it set the stage for a century of Chinese resentment toward Western interference.

Analytical Questions
  1. Multilateral Diplomacy: The Boxer Rebellion was one of the few times in the era of imperialism when the Great Powers cooperated instead of competed. Why was the “Open Door” in China important enough to bring rival empires like the U.S., Japan, and Russia together?
  2. Cultural Conflict: How did the Boxers’ belief in spiritual immunity reflect their desperation to preserve Chinese tradition against Western influence?
  3. Legacy of Intervention: In what ways did the multinational response to the Boxers reinforce the Chinese perception of Westerners as “foreign devils”?
