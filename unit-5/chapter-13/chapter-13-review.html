PART 3SECTION CHECKS · 1.1–1.4

Section Checkpoints

Choose the best answer. A short reason appears when you get one right.

13.1 Why was the 1918 flu especially damaging to the nation’s workforce and military?
Unlike ordinary flu, this strain was uniquely lethal to healthy young adults, the same group that made up the workforce and the army.
13.1 What most damaged Attorney General Palmer’s credibility and helped end the First Red Scare?
When the violent uprising Palmer predicted for May Day 1920 never happened, the public grew weary of the alarms and civil liberties advocates spoke out.
13.1 Which combination does the chapter give as the “perfect storm” behind the Red Summer?
Hundreds of thousands moved north, competed for jobs and housing, and Black veterans came home determined to claim their rights, which met violent resistance.
13.2 Why did Treasury Secretary Mellon argue for lower taxes on the wealthy?
Mellon thought wartime taxes were “scientific errors” that discouraged investment, a theory often called “trickle-down” economics.
13.2 Figure 13.4 shows the top income tax rate falling from 73 percent to 25 percent. Which result of Mellon’s program does the chapter also report?
The Bureau of the Budget helped cut spending, and Mellon used those savings to justify tax cuts that also removed many low-income Americans from the tax rolls.
13.2 What did Senator Walsh’s years of investigation into Teapot Dome show about the system of checks and balances?
Walsh untangled the shell companies and secret payments, and Fall became the first cabinet officer sent to prison for crimes committed in office.
13.3 Why did Henry Ford announce the “$5 Day” in 1914?
Ford knew mass production needed mass consumption, and higher pay also built worker loyalty and discouraged labor unions.
13.3 Figure 13.12 shows consumer credit growing from $2.6 billion in 1920 to over $7 billion in 1929 while real wages stayed fairly flat. What does this suggest?
Because pay was not keeping up, the growth of the consumer economy was fueled by debt, which could not be sustained forever.
13.3 How did radio and movies help create a “homogenized national culture”?
A family in rural Iowa could hear the same shows and follow the same heroes as a family in New York, and by 1929 over 100 million went to the movies each week.
13.4 What does the chapter call the most significant and dangerous consequence of Prohibition?
Demand for alcohol was huge and enforcement was underfunded, so gangsters like Al Capone built empires on bootlegging and speakeasies.
13.4 Why did the National Origins Act of 1924 base its quotas on the 1890 census?
The choice was deliberate: it ensured the largest quotas went to “old” groups from Northern and Western Europe such as the British and Germans.
13.4 Why was Bryan’s admission that the “six days” of creation might be “long periods of time” important? It showed that…
The modernists saw it as a victory, though Scopes was still fined $100 and the debate over science and religion did not end.
13.5 According to Figure 13.18, which of these was a “pull” that drew Black Southerners north?
The war stopped European immigration while factories ran at full capacity, so labor agents offered rail passes and high-paying jobs. The other choices were “push” factors.
13.5 How did Marcus Garvey’s approach differ from that of the NAACP?
The NAACP sought legal rights and integration through courts and The Crisis, while Garvey’s UNIA stressed pride, economic self-reliance, and independent institutions.
