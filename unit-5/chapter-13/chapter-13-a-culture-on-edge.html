Essential Question

How did the tension between modernization and traditionalism shape the American identity during a decade of Republican dominance and rapid economic change?

Americans entered the 1920s tired. In 1919 a pandemic, a wave of strikes, a hunt for radicals, and the worst racial violence in a generation arrived in the same twelve months, and in 1920 the voters chose a president who promised “normalcy.”

What followed was not quiet. This chapter follows a decade in which assembly lines, radios, and credit pulled the country into a shared modern culture while Prohibition, the Klan, immigration quotas, and the Scopes Trial showed how bitterly Americans fought over what that culture should be. It ends in Harlem, where Black writers and musicians turned the Great Migration into one of the great creative movements in American history.

Chapter 13 Key Concept

Modernization and tradition. New machines, new money, new media, and new ideas about women, race, and religion changed American life faster than many Americans were ready to accept. Each change produced a backlash, and the decade’s culture was shaped by the struggle between the two.

Arguments about immigration, about what schools should teach, about how much credit and risk the economy can bear, and about who gets to define American culture did not end in the 1920s. Seeing how those arguments began helps you judge them when you meet them today.

The Post-War Shock (1919)

Introduction

A Nation Unsettled

Americans expected the Armistice to bring relief. Instead, 1919 delivered a series of shocks that left the country anxious and divided. Section 13.1 asks how a population exhausted by war responded to disease, political fear, economic conflict and racial violence, and why it turned in 1920 to a candidate who promised calm. The section’s argument is that these crises were connected. The same insecurity that made Americans fear foreign radicals also made strikes look like revolution, and the same hostility that fueled the Red Scare helped ignite violence against Black communities.

SECTION 13.1 · PART 1Fear of Disease, Fear of Subversion

Two kinds of fear marked the months after the Armistice. The influenza pandemic killed Americans in large numbers and strained public services, while the Bolshevik Revolution in Russia and a series of bombings in 1919 produced the First Red Scare. This part examines how a public health crisis and a political panic overlapped, and how the federal government answered the second with the Palmer Raids.

Key Concepts

The Invisible Killer: The Spanish Flu Pandemic

As the guns fell silent on the battlefields of Europe in November 1918, Americans looked forward to a period of peace and healing. However, even before the war officially ended, a new and terrifying threat had begun to sweep across the globe. This was not an invading army, but a biological one. The Spanish Flu (1918 Pandemic) became one of the deadliest disasters in human history, killing significantly more people than the Great War itself. Unlike common seasonal flus that typically target the very old and very young, this particular strain of the virus was uniquely lethal to healthy young adults—the same demographic that made up the nation’s workforce and military.

The pandemic struck in three distinct waves. The first wave appeared in early 1918 and seemed relatively mild. However, the second wave, which arrived in the fall of 1918, was catastrophic. Because the United States was still fully mobilized for war, the conditions were perfect for a virus to spread. Thousands of soldiers lived in cramped barracks at training camps like Fort Devens in Massachusetts and Camp Funston in Kansas. When these men were shipped overseas on crowded transport ships, they carried the virus with them. The movement of troops acted as a global delivery system for the disease, accelerating its spread to every corner of the earth.

In the United States, the impact was devastating. Public life came to a grinding halt in many cities. Local governments issued orders to close schools, churches, and theaters to prevent the spread of the virus. In some places, it was illegal to cough or sneeze in public without covering your mouth, and citizens were required to wear gauze masks. The sheer number of deaths overwhelmed the healthcare system. Hospitals ran out of beds, and in many cities, morgues were filled to capacity. In Philadelphia, for example, the death rate was so high that horse-drawn carts patrolled the streets, with drivers calling for people to bring out their dead, much like the plagues of the Middle Ages.

The demographic impact of the pandemic was staggering. Estimates suggest that approximately 675,000 Americans died from the flu—more than ten times the number of Americans killed in combat during World War I. Globally, the death toll is estimated to be between 50 million and 100 million people. The pandemic also had a direct effect on the war effort. At one point, nearly a quarter of the U.S. Army was sick, and thousands of soldiers died of the flu before they even reached the front lines. The trauma of the pandemic, occurring simultaneously with the horrors of industrial warfare, left the American public exhausted and deeply unsettled as they entered the year 1919.

Medical science at the time was not yet advanced enough to fully understand or combat a virus. While doctors knew about bacteria, viruses were still largely mysterious. There were no antibiotics to treat secondary infections like pneumonia, and there was certainly no vaccine. Healthcare workers could do little more than provide “supportive care,” which meant keeping patients hydrated and comfortable while their bodies fought the infection. The lack of a clear medical solution contributed to the sense of panic and helplessness that gripped the nation.

The pandemic also highlighted social and economic inequalities. While the virus hit every community, crowded urban tenements and poor rural areas suffered disproportionately. In these areas, social distancing was impossible, and access to quality medical care was non-existent. The collective memory of 1919 is often dominated by political strikes and riots, but for the average American family, the year was defined by the empty chairs at the dinner table left by the invisible enemy. Families were forced to grieve in isolation, as traditional funeral rites were often prohibited to prevent further contagion. This private suffering, occurring on a mass scale, created a profound psychological rift in the American experience, making the transition back to civilian life incredibly difficult for returning veterans and their families alike.

Key Concepts

The First Red Scare: Hysteria and the Palmer Raids

While the pandemic ravaged the population, a different kind of fear began to take root in the American psyche: the fear of political subversion. This period, known as the First Red Scare, was a wave of intense anti-communist paranoia that swept the country in 1919 and 1920. The roots of this hysteria lay in the Bolshevik Revolution of 1917 in Russia. Led by Vladimir Lenin, the Bolsheviks had overthrown the Russian monarchy and established a communist state. They called for a worldwide revolution of the working class to topple capitalist governments everywhere.

To many Americans, this was a direct threat to the “American Way of Life”—a system based on private property, individual liberty, and democratic government. The fear was intensified by a series of events in 1919 that seemed to suggest a communist plot was underway in the United States. A series of mail bombs were sent to prominent government and business leaders, including John D. Rockefeller and Supreme Court Justice Oliver Wendell Holmes. One bomb even exploded at the home of the U.S. Attorney General, A. Mitchell Palmer.

Palmer, who had presidential ambitions, used these attacks to justify a massive government crackdown on suspected radicals. He appointed J. Edgar Hoover to head a new anti-radical division within the Justice Department (which would later become the FBI). Together, they organized a series of coordinated attacks known as the Palmer Raids. Throughout late 1919 and early 1920, federal agents burst into the meeting halls and homes of suspected communists, socialists, and anarchists. Thousands of people were arrested, often without warrants and held for long periods without being charged with a crime.

The Red Scare was deeply intertwined with nativism—the suspicion of immigrants and foreign influences. Many of the people targeted in the Palmer Raids were immigrants who had not yet become citizens. The government used the Alien Act of 1918 to deport hundreds of people based on their political beliefs rather than any specific illegal actions. The most famous example of this was the deportation of Emma Goldman, a well-known anarchist and feminist who had lived in the U.S. for decades. She, along with 248 others, was put on a ship nicknamed the “Soviet Ark” and sent back to Russia.

The atmosphere of suspicion extended into the legal system. One of the most controversial cases of the era involved Sacco and Vanzetti, two Italian immigrants and self-proclaimed anarchists. They were arrested in 1920 for the robbery and murder of a factory paymaster and a guard in South Braintree, Massachusetts. Despite weak evidence and multiple witnesses providing alibis for the men, they were convicted and sentenced to death. Many observers believed they were convicted more for their radical political views and their immigrant status than for any actual evidence of the crime. Their case became a global cause célèbre, symbolizing the perceived injustice of the Red Scare era.

The First Red Scare eventually burned itself out. Palmer’s credibility suffered a major blow when he predicted a massive, violent uprising on May Day (International Workers’ Day) in 1920 that never happened. As the predicted violence failed to materialize, the public began to grow weary of the constant alarms. Furthermore, prominent lawyers and civil liberties advocates began to speak out against the government’s disregard for due process and the Bill of Rights. While the intense hysteria faded, the period left a lasting legacy of government surveillance and a deep-seated suspicion of labor unions and radical politics. The Scare effectively marginalized political dissent for a decade, ensuring that the pro-business policies of the 1920s would meet little organized resistance from the political left.

Key Concepts

Labor Unrest and the Post-War Economy

The year 1919 was also defined by an unprecedented wave of labor strikes. During the war, the government had worked closely with labor unions to ensure continuous production. In exchange for a “no-strike” pledge, unions saw their memberships grow and their wages increase. However, once the war ended, the government lifted price controls, and inflation skyrocketed. The cost of living doubled, but wages remained stagnant. Furthermore, business leaders, no longer under pressure to maintain wartime production, moved to roll back the gains unions had made, such as the eight-hour workday.

The result was a massive explosion of industrial conflict. Over 3,600 strikes occurred in 1919, involving more than 4 million workers. Two of the most significant were the Seattle General Strike and the Boston Police Strike. In Seattle, more than 60,000 workers walked off the job, bringing the entire city to a standstill for five days. This was the first “general strike” in American history, where workers from many different industries struck together in solidarity. Although the strike was peaceful, the mayor of Seattle and many national newspapers labeled it a “Bolshevik” attempt to start a revolution, further fueling the Red Scare.

The Boston Police Strike of September 1919 had an even greater political impact. Boston police officers had not received a raise in years and were working in deteriorating conditions. When they attempted to form a union and went on strike, the city was left without police protection, and some looting occurred. The Governor of Massachusetts, Calvin Coolidge, took a hardline stance. He called in the National Guard to restore order and famously declared, “There is no right to strike against the public safety by anybody, anywhere, any time.”

Coolidge fired all the striking officers and replaced them with new recruits. His firm handling of the situation made him a national hero among conservatives and eventually propelled him to the vice presidency and then the presidency. The failure of the 1919 strikes was a major setback for organized labor. Business leaders used the public’s fear of radicalism to associate unions with communism, a tactic that would keep the labor movement on the defensive throughout the 1920s.

The transition from a wartime to a peacetime economy was rocky. The sudden cancellation of billions of dollars in war contracts led to a brief but sharp recession. Returning soldiers struggled to find jobs, often competing with African Americans and women who had entered the industrial workforce during the war. This economic competition, combined with the underlying racial tensions of the era, created a volatile social environment that was ready to explode. The lack of a clear federal plan for demobilization meant that millions of Americans were left to navigate a rapidly changing economy with little support, leading to deep-seated resentment that often found an outlet in social conflict.

Key Concepts

The Red Summer: Racial Violence and the New Negro

As if the pandemic, the Red Scare, and labor unrest were not enough, 1919 also witnessed some of the worst racial violence in American history. This period became known as the Red Summer (1919), a term coined by NAACP leader James Weldon Johnson to describe the blood that flowed in dozens of American cities. The violence was the result of a “perfect storm” of factors: the Great Migration, economic competition, and the changed expectations of African American veterans.

During the war, hundreds of thousands of African Americans had moved from the rural South to the industrial North in search of jobs and an escape from Jim Crow laws. This demographic shift created intense competition for housing and employment in northern cities. Furthermore, about 400,000 African American men had served in the military during World War I. Having fought “to make the world safe for democracy” in Europe, these veterans returned home with a new sense of pride and a determination to claim their full rights as citizens. This assertive attitude was often met with violent resistance from white citizens who wanted to maintain the pre-war racial status quo.

The violence erupted in more than two dozen cities. The Chicago Race Riot was one of the most severe. It began when a Black teenager, Eugene Williams, accidentally drifted into the “white” section of a segregated beach on Lake Michigan. White bathers threw rocks at him, causing him to drown. When the police refused to arrest the man identified as the rock-thrower, fighting broke out between Black and white crowds. The violence lasted for nearly a week, resulting in 38 deaths and leaving over 1,000 families—mostly Black—homeless.

What distinguished the Red Summer from previous eras of racial violence was the level of Black resistance. African Americans did not simply flee; they fought back. In Washington D.C., Black veterans organized armed patrols to protect their neighborhoods from white mobs. This spirit of defiance was part of a broader cultural and political movement often called the “New Negro” movement. It signaled that the era of passive submission to racial violence was over.

Although it occurred slightly later, the Tulsa Race Massacre (1921) represented the extreme peak of this era’s racial hostility. In the early 20th century, the Greenwood district of Tulsa, Oklahoma, was one of the most prosperous African American communities in the nation, often called the “Black Wall Street.” In June 1921, following an unproven accusation against a Black man involving a white woman, white mobs—some of them deputized and armed by city officials—invaded Greenwood.

Over the course of 48 hours, the mobs burned more than 35 square blocks of the district to the ground. Private airplanes were reportedly used to drop incendiary devices on the neighborhood. While the official death toll at the time was listed as 36, modern historians believe as many as 300 people were killed. Thousands were left homeless and held in internment camps. For decades, the massacre was largely omitted from history books and public discussion, representing a profound collective failure to address the depths of American racial trauma. The massacre destroyed not just buildings, but the economic foundation of a generation of Black Tulsans, illustrating the high cost of African American success in a society dominated by white supremacy.

Key Concepts

The Search for Stability: Harding and the “Return to Normalcy”

By the end of 1919, the American public was exhausted. The idealism of Woodrow Wilson’s “Fourteen Points” had been replaced by the grim realities of a pandemic, domestic terrorism, labor strikes, and race riots. Americans were tired of reform, tired of sacrifice, and tired of international responsibilities. They craved stability and a sense of the familiar.

In the election of 1920, the Republican candidate, Warren G. Harding, captured this national mood perfectly. Harding was a genial senator from Ohio who lacked the intellectual depth of Wilson but possessed a comforting presence. His campaign slogan was a promise of a “Return to Normalcy”. While “normalcy” wasn’t actually a word in the dictionary at the time, everyone understood what Harding meant. He meant a return to a time before the war, before the Progressive reforms, and before the world became so complicated.

Harding’s vision of normalcy included a retreat from international entanglements (isolationism) and a return to pro-business economic policies (laissez-faire). His overwhelming victory—winning over 60% of the popular vote—signaled the end of the Progressive Era and the beginning of a decade of Republican dominance. The American people had decided that they were done trying to change the world; they just wanted to enjoy the benefits of peace and prosperity. However, as the 1920s would prove, the forces of modernization had already changed the country forever, and there was no true way to go back to the world as it was before 1914. Harding’s presidency would mark the start of a period where business interests and social traditionalism would define the national agenda, even as the undercurrents of the 1919 shocks continued to vibrate beneath the surface of American life.

DATA AND DEBATEThe Invisible Enemy

The Evidence

If we look at the death statistics for the year 1918–1919, we see a startling comparison. United States combat deaths in World War I totaled approximately 53,000. In contrast, deaths from the Spanish Flu (1918 Pandemic) in the U.S. are estimated at approximately 675,000. This data reveals that for every American soldier who died from a German bullet or shell, more than 12 Americans died from a microscopic virus. The pandemic was mathematically a much larger disaster for the American population than the war itself. Furthermore, the death rate for the flu was highest among people aged 20 to 40, which is typically the group most resistant to disease.

What the Data Reveals The demographic skew toward healthy young adults significantly impacted the U.S. workforce and military readiness. Troop movements, both domestic and international, acted as a primary vector for the disease. For example, the crowded conditions at Camp Funston in Kansas and the subsequent transit of those soldiers to the Western Front provided an ideal environment for the virus to mutate and spread.

The Historical Debate

Historians often debate why the pandemic is frequently treated as a “footnote” in history books compared to the massive amount of space dedicated to the military history of World War I. One theory is that war fits into a traditional narrative of “heroism” and “national purpose,” while a pandemic feels like a senseless, random tragedy that people would rather forget. Another theory is that because the pandemic happened simultaneously with the end of the war, the two events became blurred in the public’s memory.

Why It Matters

Understanding the scale of the pandemic helps us understand the profound “exhaustion” of the American public in 1919. It wasn’t just war weariness; it was a deep psychological trauma caused by a year where death was present in every neighborhood, not just on a distant battlefield. This collective trauma directly contributed to the desire for a “Return to Normalcy” and the xenophobia of the First Red Scare.

SECTION 13.2 · PART 1The Transition from Reform to Recovery

The dawn of the 1920s found the American public in a state of profound psychological and political exhaustion. For nearly two decades, the nation had been driven by the relentless energy of the Progressive Era, a time defined by intense social activism, trust-busting, and the expansion of federal power to address the ills of industrialization. This era of domestic reform had been immediately followed by the cataclysmic trauma of World War I, which demanded total national mobilization, the suspension of certain civil liberties, and the sacrifice of over 100,000 American lives. When the war ended, it did not bring immediate peace, but rather the “Post-War Shock” of 1919—a year of deadly disease, labor strikes, and racial violence. By the time the presidential election of 1920 arrived, the American electorate was no longer interested in “making the world safe for democracy” or pursuing further radical changes to the social order.

The Democratic candidate in 1920, James M. Cox, attempted to carry the mantle of Woodrow Wilson, urging Americans to embrace the League of Nations and continue the spirit of international cooperation and domestic reform. However, the public mood had shifted decisively against Wilsonian idealism. Voters perceived the outgoing administration as overly intellectual, heavy-handed in its regulations, and too focused on foreign entanglements. They craved a leader who would let them focus on their own lives, their own businesses, and their own communities. They wanted a respite from the “high-strung” atmosphere of the previous twenty years.

Key Concepts

Defining the New Era

Into this climate stepped the Republican candidate, Warren G. Harding. A senator from Ohio with a genial personality and a stately appearance, Harding was the perfect visual representation of stability. He lacked the intellectual depth or the fiery oratory of Wilson or Theodore Roosevelt, but he possessed a comforting, “neighborly” quality that made him feel safe to a weary public. During the campaign, Harding famously promised a “Return to Normalcy”. Although “normalcy” was a linguistic quirk—the standard word was “normality”—the term perfectly captured the national desire to retreat into a simpler, more predictable past. Harding argued that the nation needed “not heroics, but healing; not nostrums, but normalcy; not revolution, but restoration.” This message resonated so deeply that Harding won the election in a landslide, capturing 60 percent of the popular vote and signaling a dramatic shift in the direction of the American government.

The concept of a “Return to Normalcy” was not just a campaign slogan; it was a comprehensive philosophy that guided the first half of the decade. At its core, it represented a rejection of the “activist government” model of the Progressives. Harding and his supporters believed that the federal government had become too large, too expensive, and too intrusive during the war years. To “return to normal,” the government had to shrink its footprint and restore the traditional balance between the public and private sectors. This meant cutting federal spending, reducing the national debt that had ballooned during the war, and dismantling the many wartime agencies that still regulated everything from fuel to food.

This shift also marked the beginning of a period of Republican dominance in Washington. The party would hold the presidency for the next twelve years, and throughout the 1920s, they controlled both houses of Congress. This political monopoly allowed them to implement a pro-business agenda with very little opposition. The goal was to create a “business-friendly” environment where the primary role of the state was to facilitate economic growth rather than to serve as a social referee. This philosophy would redefine the American landscape, ushering in a decade of unprecedented industrial expansion, but also setting the stage for significant political and economic vulnerabilities.

SECTION 13.2 · PART 2Mellon’s Economics: The Laissez-Faire Revival

The economic engine of the Harding and Coolidge administrations was driven by a return to laissez-faire principles. This “hands-off” approach to the economy was based on the belief that the market functions best when it is free from government interference. The chief architect of this policy was Harding’s Secretary of the Treasury, Andrew Mellon, one of the wealthiest men in the world and a prominent banker. Mellon believed that the high taxes imposed during the war were “scientific errors” that discouraged wealthy individuals from investing in new businesses. He argued that if the government lowered taxes on the rich, they would use that extra capital to build factories and create jobs, which would eventually benefit everyone—a theory often called “trickle-down” economics.

Mellon’s plan had three primary goals: to reduce the national debt, to cut federal spending, and to lower taxes. To achieve the first two goals, the administration supported the creation of the Bureau of the Budget in 1921. For the first time, the federal government had a centralized agency to track and control spending across all departments. Under the direction of Charles Dawes, the bureau was remarkably successful in its early years, helping to slash the federal budget from $6.4 billion in 1920 to $3.3 billion by 1923. Mellon used these savings to justify a series of massive tax cuts. By 1925, the top income tax rate had been cut from 73 percent to 25 percent, and many low-income Americans were removed from the tax rolls entirely.

Protective Walls and Global Consequences

While the administration promoted a “free market” at home, it took a very different approach toward international trade. To protect American manufacturers and farmers from the competition of a recovering Europe, the Republican-led Congress passed the Fordney-McCumber Tariff in 1922. This law raised duties on imported goods to their highest levels in American history, with some rates reaching 60 percent. The tariff was highly popular with American factory owners, who no longer had to worry about cheaper foreign products undercutting their prices.

However, the Fordney-McCumber Tariff had disastrous unintended consequences for the global economy. Most European nations, particularly Britain and France, were struggling to rebuild their war-torn industries and were deep in debt to the United States. To pay back these war loans, they needed to sell their products in the American market to earn U.S. dollars. By closing off that market, the tariff made it nearly impossible for the Allies to settle their debts. In response, many European nations raised their own tariffs against American goods, leading to a stifling of international trade. This economic nationalism contributed to a cycle of instability that would eventually make the global economy more vulnerable to the shocks of the late 1920s.

The Plight of the American Farmer

Despite the general prosperity of the “Coolidge Era,” one major sector of the economy was left behind: agriculture. During World War I, the government had encouraged farmers to increase production to feed the troops, leading many to take out large loans to buy more land and new machinery. When the war ended and European agriculture recovered, the global demand for American crops plummeted. Prices fell sharply, and many farmers found themselves unable to pay back their debts. While Mellon’s tax cuts benefited industrial titans and urban investors, they did little for the rural families facing foreclosure. The government’s commitment to laissez-faire meant that the Republican administrations were largely unwilling to provide direct federal aid to farmers, believing that the “law of supply and demand” would eventually fix the problem.

SECTION 13.2 · PART 3The Ohio Gang and the Culture of Corruption

Warren G. Harding was a man who valued loyalty and friendship above almost all else. When he moved into the White House, he brought with him a group of political associates from his home state who became known as the Ohio Gang. These were the men Harding felt most comfortable with—men he had played poker with in the backrooms of Ohio politics. He appointed them to powerful positions throughout the federal government, often ignoring their lack of qualifications or their questionable reputations. While Harding himself was generally considered an honest man, he was also a weak administrator who preferred to delegate authority rather than oversee it. This combination of loyalty and negligence created a “wild west” atmosphere in Washington, where government offices were treated as personal “piggy banks” for the president’s friends.

The corruption of the Ohio Gang was systemic and touched almost every corner of the administration. Charles Forbes, the head of the Veterans’ Bureau, was caught in a massive graft scheme, pocketing millions of dollars intended for the construction of hospitals for wounded soldiers. The Alien Property Custodian was found taking bribes to return seized German assets to their former owners. Even the Attorney General, Harry Daugherty, was investigated for his involvement in the illegal sale of liquor permits during Prohibition. The administration’s headquarters for these activities was often a small house on K Street, where the Ohio Gang gathered to drink, play cards, and trade political favors for cash. Harding, who enjoyed these gatherings, seemed blissfully or willfully unaware of the scale of the looting happening under his nose.

Teapot Dome: The Scandal of the Century

The most significant and lasting stain on the Harding administration was the Teapot Dome Scandal. This case involved the illegal leasing of federal oil reserves and became the first time in American history that a cabinet official was sent to prison for crimes committed while in office. The scandal centered on lands at Teapot Dome, Wyoming, and Elk Hills, California, which had been set aside as naval oil reserves. These reserves were meant to be kept in the ground, ensuring that the U.S. Navy would have a guaranteed fuel supply in the event of a national emergency or war.

In 1921, the Secretary of the Interior, Albert B. Fall, a close friend of the Ohio Gang, convinced Harding to transfer control of these oil-rich lands from the Navy Department to the Interior Department. Once he had control, Fall secretly leased the land to two private oil companies—Pan American Petroleum and Mammoth Oil—without any competitive bidding. In exchange for these lucrative leases, Fall received more than $400,000 in “loans,” cash, and bonds—an amount equivalent to several million dollars today. The scandal came to light through the persistent investigation of Senator Thomas J. Walsh, who spent years untangling the complex web of shell companies and secret payments Fall had used to hide the bribes.

Constitutional Implications

The Teapot Dome scandal was more than just a case of simple bribery; it was a profound constitutional crisis that tested the limits of executive power and the effectiveness of Congressional oversight. It revealed how easily the nation’s natural resources could be exploited for private gain if the president failed to exercise proper supervision over his subordinates. The scandal served as a grim reminder that the “normalcy” Harding promised could easily mask a culture of greed and lawlessness.

Harding did not live to see the full exposure of his friends’ crimes. In the summer of 1923, while on a speaking tour of the West, Harding died suddenly of a heart attack or stroke in San Francisco. While the nation initially mourned him as a kind and gentle leader, his reputation collapsed as the details of the Teapot Dome and the Ohio Gang’s activities were revealed during the subsequent investigations. Harding is now frequently ranked by historians as one of the least effective presidents in American history, a man whose personal decency was overwhelmed by his poor judgment and the criminal ambitions of his inner circle.

SECTION 13.2 · PART 4“Silent Cal” and the Business of America

Upon Harding’s death, the presidency fell to Vice President Calvin Coolidge. The contrast between the two men could not have been more striking. If Harding was gregarious and easily influenced, Coolidge was a man of legendary reserve and uncompromising integrity. A native of Vermont, Coolidge embodied the traditional New England virtues of thrift, silence, and emotional restraint, earning him the nickname “Silent Cal.” His first act as president was to be sworn in by his own father, a justice of the peace, by the light of a kerosene lamp in the family’s farmhouse—a scene that perfectly projected the image of a simple, honest man who would restore dignity to the White House.

Coolidge’s political philosophy was even more deeply rooted in the concept of limited government than Harding’s. He famously declared that “the chief business of the American people is business.” Coolidge believed that the government’s primary role was to stay out of the way of private enterprise and allow the “natural laws” of capitalism to create prosperity. Under his leadership, the spirit of deregulation reached its peak. He appointed individuals to federal regulatory agencies—such as the Federal Trade Commission and the Interstate Commerce Commission—who were actually opposed to the very idea of regulation. These agencies, which had been created during the Progressive Era to protect the public, began to function more as partners and advocates for the industries they were supposed to oversee.

The Era of “Coolidge Prosperity”

The mid-1920s were a time of rapid economic expansion that many Americans credited to Coolidge’s “hands-off” policies. Supported by Mellon’s continued tax cuts and a booming stock market, the nation entered a period of unprecedented consumerism. Industrial production soared, the middle class expanded, and for the first time, millions of Americans were able to afford luxuries like automobiles and electrical appliances. Farmers were producing more than the market could consume, leading to a persistent surplus and falling prices. Key Concepts

Isolationism and the Disarmament Movement

While the Republican administrations focused on promoting business at home, they pursued a foreign policy defined by isolationism. This was not a policy of total withdrawal from the world—the United States remained a global leader in trade and finance—but it was a firm rejection of the “collective security” model championed by Woodrow Wilson. The American public was deeply disillusioned by the outcome of World War I, believing that the nation had been tricked into the conflict by arms manufacturers and international bankers. There was a widespread determination never again to be dragged into a “European war” or to be bound by the rules of an international body like the League of Nations.

The primary goal of U.S. diplomacy in the 1920s was to maintain world peace through legal agreements and disarmament rather than through military alliances. In 1921, the U.S. hosted the Washington Naval Conference, the first international arms control conference in history. The major naval powers—the U.S., Britain, Japan, France, and Italy—agreed to scrap many of their largest warships and to limit the future construction of battleships according to a specific ratio (5:5:3 for the U.S., Britain, and Japan). While the conference was hailed as a major victory for peace, it had significant flaws. It only limited large ships, leading to a race to build smaller vessels like cruisers and submarines, and it lacked any mechanism to enforce the limits if a nation decided to cheat.

The most idealistic expression of this “peace through law” movement was the Kellogg-Briand Pact of 1928. Negotiated by U.S. Secretary of State Frank B. The U.S. also had to address the massive financial mess left by the war. Germany was unable to pay the crushing reparations demanded by the Treaty of Versailles, which in turn prevented Britain and France from paying back their war loans to the United States. To prevent a total collapse of the European economy, American banker Charles G. Dawes negotiated the Dawes Plan in 1924. Under this plan, American banks loaned money to Germany, which Germany used to pay its reparations to Britain and France. Britain and France, in turn, used that same money to pay back their debts to the U.S. government. While the plan temporarily stabilized the situation, it created a fragile financial “merry-go-round” that depended entirely on the continued flow of American credit. If the American economy were to falter, the entire global financial structure would come crashing down—a risk that would become a reality at the end of the decade.

SECTION 13.3 · PART 1The Architect of the New Era: Henry Ford and the Factory

The economic prosperity of the 1920s was not a random occurrence but the result of a fundamental shift in how goods were made and sold. At the center of this transformation was Henry Ford, a man whose vision for the manufacturing process would alter the course of global history. Before the 20th century, many products, including early automobiles, were luxury items handcrafted by skilled artisans. In these early workshops, a small team of mechanics would build a car from the ground up, fitting each part by hand. This process was slow, expensive, and limited the market to the very wealthy. Ford’s genius lay in his realization that if he could simplify the manufacturing process and lower the cost of the final product, he could create a mass market of consumers from the working class.

In 1908, Ford introduced the Model T, a vehicle designed to be sturdy, reliable, and, above all, affordable. To achieve the price point he desired, Ford revolutionized the factory floor by implementing the Assembly Line. This system broke down the complex task of building a car into hundreds of simple, repetitive steps. Rather than workers moving to the car, the car moved to the workers on a motorized conveyor belt. Each man stayed in one spot and performed a single task—such as tightening a specific bolt or attaching a fender—over and over again. This innovation in Mass Production allowed Ford to reduce the time it took to build a single Model T from over twelve hours to just ninety-three minutes. As production speed increased, the price plummeted from $850 in 1908 to less than $300 by the mid-1920s. By making the car cheap enough for his own workers to buy, Ford transformed the automobile from a luxury plaything into a tool of universal mobility.

Ford’s impact extended beyond the mechanics of the factory. He understood that mass production required mass consumption; if his workers could not afford to buy the cars they were building, his business model would eventually fail. In 1914, he shocked the industrial world by announcing the “$5 Day,” which more than doubled the average wage of an industrial worker at the time. This policy was a cornerstone of what became known as Welfare Capitalism. By providing higher wages, shorter work weeks, and even benefits like healthcare and recreation programs, Ford and other industrial leaders hoped to build worker loyalty, discourage the formation of labor unions, and ensure that their employees had enough disposable income to participate in the burgeoning consumer market.

However, this high pay came with a high level of corporate control. Ford created a “Sociology Department” tasked with monitoring the private lives of his employees. Inspectors would visit workers’ homes to ensure they were living “clean” lives, avoiding alcohol and gambling, and maintaining a proper domestic environment. If a worker failed to meet these moral standards, they could lose their $5-a-day bonus. The work itself was also grueling; the assembly line was mind-numbing and physically exhausting, leading to high turnover rates. To Ford, the worker was an interchangeable part of the machine, a philosophy that prioritized efficiency over individual craftsmanship. Despite these tensions, the lure of high wages drew millions to Detroit, turning it into the industrial heartbeat of the world.

Key Concepts

The Automobile and the Remaking of America

The explosion of automobile ownership had ripple effects that reached far beyond the walls of the Ford Motor Company. By 1929, there were over 26 million cars on the road in the United States—approximately one car for every five people. The automobile was no longer a toy for the rich; it had become a necessity for the middle class. This rapid adoption of the car necessitated a massive expansion of the nation’s infrastructure. In the early 1900s, most American roads were dirt tracks that turned into impassable mud during the rain. The 1920s saw the true birth of the modern highway system. Federal legislation, such as the Federal Aid Road Act, provided matching funds to states to build paved highways. Thousands of miles of concrete and asphalt were laid down, connecting once-isolated rural communities to the bustling urban centers.

This new mobility gave rise to a host of related industries that powered the national economy. The demand for tires led to a boom in the rubber industry; the need for windshields sparked growth in the glass industry; and the requirement for engines drove the steel and oil sectors to new heights. Thousands of new jobs were created in gas stations, repair shops, and motels. The oil industry, in particular, underwent a massive expansion as the nation’s thirst for gasoline became insatiable. This industrial synergy created a positive feedback loop: as the car industry grew, it pulled the rest of the economy up with it, providing the foundation for the “Coolidge Prosperity” of the mid-decade.

Perhaps most significantly, the car transformed the physical landscape through the phenomenon of Urban Sprawl. Before the automobile, most people lived in “walking cities,” where they resided close to their place of work or near a streetcar line. With a car, people were no longer restricted by geography. Middle-class families began to move away from the crowded, noisy, and often polluted city centers to new residential developments on the outskirts. These suburbs offered a quieter lifestyle, larger yards, and a sense of escape. However, this movement also began the long process of hollowing out the economic and social cores of many American cities, as wealth and tax revenue moved to the periphery. The suburb became the new American ideal, a hybrid of urban convenience and rural peace.

The automobile also birthed a new era of American tourism and leisure. For the first time, families could pack up their belongings and travel across the country on their own schedule. This “democratization of travel” led to the rise of roadside diners and “tourist cabins”—the precursors to the modern motel. The car provided a new sense of freedom and privacy, particularly for young people, who used the vehicle as a way to escape the watchful eyes of their parents and chaperones. This social shift would contribute to the changing moral landscape of the decade, as the car became a mobile space for dating and independence. The “Sunday Drive” became a secular ritual, a way for families to explore the changing American landscape and participate in the shared experience of modern life.

SECTION 13.3 · PART 2The Rise of Consumerism and the Advertising Industry

As the efficiency of the assembly line filled warehouses with a seemingly endless supply of goods, American business faced a new challenge: how to convince people to keep buying. This period marked the birth of modern Consumerism, a cultural shift where people began to define their social status and personal happiness through the acquisition of material possessions. To facilitate this, the Advertising Industry underwent a radical transformation. No longer did ads simply list the features and prices of a product in a straightforward, informative manner. Instead, advertisers began to use the principles of modern psychology to tap into the consumer’s deepest desires, fears, and insecurities.

Advertisers in the 1920s didn’t just sell soap; they sold “beauty,” “youth,” and “social acceptance.” They didn’t just sell mouthwash; they sold the fear of being a social outcast due to “halitosis.” The legendary ad campaign for Listerine turned a minor medical condition into a source of deep social anxiety, convincing millions that they were one bad breath away from losing their friends or their chance at romance. Celebrity endorsements became common, as movie stars and athletes were used to lend glamour and authority to everyday products. This barrage of imagery created a sense of “perpetual dissatisfaction,” where consumers felt they needed the latest model or the newest gadget to stay current and respected.

To ensure that the demand for goods never ceased, companies began to implement the practice of Planned Obsolescence. This was the intentional design of products to wear out or become stylistically outdated after a few years. If a consumer’s car still ran perfectly but the new model had more stylish fenders or a different color, the consumer was encouraged to feel that their current vehicle was obsolete. This cycle of “new and improved” versions of the same basic products ensured that the factory wheels kept turning. The consumer was no longer a person with needs to be met, but a “market” to be managed and manipulated through sophisticated psychological techniques.

The center of this new consumer world was the American home, which was being transformed by the rapid expansion of the electrical grid. By the end of the 1920s, approximately 70 percent of American households had electricity. This spurred a massive demand for Electrical Appliances that promised to make domestic life easier and more efficient. Vacuum cleaners, washing machines, electric irons, and refrigerators became standard features in middle-class houses. While these inventions did reduce the physical toll of housework, they often did not decrease the amount of time women spent on domestic labor. Instead, the “standard” for cleanliness rose, and women were expected to manage increasingly complex households without the help of domestic servants. The advertising of these appliances often targeted women directly, framing the purchase of a vacuum cleaner as an act of “love” for the family or a way to show one’s status as a modern, sophisticated housewife.

A Homogenized National Culture: The Impact of Mass Media

For most of American history, culture was intensely local and regional. People listened to the music of their community, read local newspapers that focused on nearby events, and participated in traditions that varied from state to state. The 1920s shattered this isolation through the rise of mass media, which created a shared national experience for the first time. The most revolutionary of these new technologies was the radio. In 1920, KDKA in Pittsburgh became the first commercial radio station to go on the air, broadcasting the results of the presidential election. Within a few short years, millions of Americans were tuning in to the same programs, the same news reports, and the same advertisements.

The radio created a “simultaneous experience” for the nation. A family in rural Iowa could listen to the same jazz concert, the same comedy program, or the same baseball game as a family in New York City. This contributed to a more homogenized national culture, as regional accents began to soften and shared national “heroes” emerged. Sports figures like Babe Ruth and aviators like Charles Lindbergh became household names not just because of their achievements, but because their stories were broadcast directly into every living room. This shared cultural landscape provided a sense of unity in a country that was otherwise deeply divided by race, religion, and class. The radio also became a powerful tool for political communication, allowing leaders to speak directly to the people without the filter of local newspapers.

At the same time, the motion picture industry was becoming a dominant force in American life. In the early years of the decade, movies were silent, relying on expressive acting and title cards to tell stories. Stars like Charlie Chaplin became global icons, his slapstick humor and “Little Tramp” persona transcending language barriers and cultural differences. Hollywood became the dream factory of the world, churning out thousands of films that established the “American Way” as a global standard for style, behavior, and aspiration. The movies provided a common vocabulary of slang, fashion, and social norms that were adopted by young people across the country.

The ultimate turning point for the industry came in 1927 with the release of The Jazz Singer, the first “talking movie” or “talkie.” The addition of sound revolutionized the cinematic experience, making movies even more immersive and popular. By 1929, over 100 million Americans went to the movies every week—nearly the entire population. This mass participation in the cinema further eroded local differences and created a national culture of celebrity. While the content of many films—often focusing on wealth, romance, and rebellion—scandalized traditionalists in rural areas, they were irresistible to a public that was eager to escape the mundane realities of everyday life and participate in the glamour of the “Roaring Twenties.” The “silver screen” became a mirror in which Americans saw a version of themselves that was more exciting, more affluent, and more modern than their daily reality.

SECTION 13.3 · PART 3The Credit Economy and the Risks of Prosperity

The most significant—and ultimately the most dangerous—shift in the 1920s economy was the way Americans paid for their new modern lives. For generations, the American value system had emphasized thrift, saving, and “paying your way.” Borrowing money for non-essential items was seen as a sign of failure or a lack of character. However, to sustain the high levels of mass production coming off the assembly lines, business leaders needed to find a way for people to buy goods they could not afford upfront. The solution was the Installment Plan.

Under an installment plan, a consumer could “buy now and pay later.” They would make a small down payment—often called a “dollar down”—and then pay off the remainder of the cost in monthly increments, plus interest. This “easy credit” was applied to everything from Model Ts to radios and washing machines. By 1929, over 60 percent of automobiles and 80 percent of radios were purchased on credit. While this allowed the middle class to enjoy a much higher standard of living in the short term, it also led to a massive increase in household debt. Many families were living on the edge of their means, their entire lifestyle dependent on a steady paycheck that could never be interrupted. The “good life” of the 1920s was, for many, a life built on a foundation of borrowed money.

This culture of credit extended to the financial markets through a practice known as Buying on Margin. During the 1920s, the stock market was seen as a “sure bet” for wealth. Many ordinary Americans—not just wealthy bankers—began to invest in stocks, but they didn’t use their own savings. Instead, they would pay a small percentage of the stock’s price (the margin), sometimes as little as 10 percent, and borrow the rest from a broker. The broker, in turn, borrowed the money from a bank. As long as stock prices continued to rise, the investor could sell the stock, pay back the loan, and keep a handsome profit. This created an artificial demand for stocks, driving prices higher and higher, far beyond the actual value of the companies.

This speculative bubble created a “paper wealth” that masked deep structural weaknesses in the economy. While the Republican administrations and business leaders celebrated the “Coolidge Prosperity” as a permanent state of affairs, the underlying reality was that the economy was becoming increasingly top-heavy and fragile. The gap between the wealthy and the working class was widening, and the agricultural sector was already in a state of collapse. The consumer economy of the 1920s was a magnificent achievement of industrial logic, but it carried within it the seeds of its own destruction. When the supply of easy credit eventually dried up and stock prices began to falter, the entire “house of cards” would come crashing down, transforming a decade of roaring growth into a decade of unprecedented despair.

If we examine the economic data from 1920 to 1929, we see a dramatic divergence in American spending habits. At the same time, the percentage of the nation’s wealth held by the top 1% of the population rose significantly, while real wages for factory workers remained relatively flat compared to the increase in industrial productivity.

What the Data Reveals

This data shows that the growth of the consumer economy was directly fueled by the growth of debt. The explosion in auto ownership—a centerpiece of the decade’s prosperity—would have been impossible without the introduction of the Installment Plan. Furthermore, the data reveals a “saturation point”; by the late 1920s, almost every family that could afford a car or a radio (even on credit) already had one. This led to a slowdown in demand that the factory system was not prepared to handle. The “prosperity” of the era was mathematically dependent on a continuous expansion of debt that could not be sustained indefinitely.

The Historical Debate

Historians and economists often debate whether the “credit boom” of the 1920s was the primary cause of the Great Depression or merely a symptom of a larger problem. Some argue that the installment plan was a revolutionary tool that democratized wealth and allowed the middle class to thrive, suggesting the crash was caused by poor government regulation of the banks. Others contend that it created a “false prosperity” that masked the fact that real wages for most workers were not keeping up with the increase in production. They suggest that the economy of the 1920s was like a “house of cards” that was destined to fall once the supply of easy credit dried up and the gap between production and consumption became too wide.

SECTION 13.4 · PART 1The Emergence of the “New Woman” and the Break from Tradition

The decade following World War I was marked by a profound psychological shift in American life, nowhere more visible than in the changing roles and behaviors of women. For decades, the late 19th-century Victorian era had imposed a strict moral and social code known as the “Cult of Domesticity.” This philosophy argued that women were the natural moral guardians of the home, a “private sphere” that was to remain separate from the corrupting influences of the “public sphere” of politics and business. Women were expected to be pious, submissive, and physically modest, constrained by heavy corsets and floor-length skirts that symbolized their restricted social mobility. However, the cataclysm of the Great War and the hard-won victory of the 19th Amendment in 1920 shattered these traditional boundaries, giving rise to a new generation of women determined to live on their own terms.

This revolutionary social figure became known as The Flapper. The flapper was more than just a fashion trend; she was a walking protest against the restrictions of the past. Her appearance was a deliberate rejection of Victorian femininity. Gone were the long tresses and restrictive undergarments; in their place were bobbed hair, short “shift” dresses that ended at the knee, and a profusion of cosmetics like rouge and lipstick, which had previously been associated only with women of “low character.” The flapper’s silhouette was intentionally boyish, reflecting a desire for physical freedom and a move away from the exaggerated curves of the previous century. This change in dress allowed women to participate in the energetic, fast-paced culture of the Jazz Age, specifically the Charleston, a dance that emphasized athletic movement and personal expression over the formal, structured ballroom dances of the past.

Beyond fashion, the flapper movement represented a fundamental change in social behavior and morality. Young urban women began to engage in activities that had once been the exclusive domain of men. They smoked cigarettes in public, frequented urban clubs to listen to jazz, and drank illegal alcohol alongside their male counterparts. This shift was fueled by a new sense of economic independence. By 1930, nearly 10 million women were in the workforce, representing a 25 percent increase from the beginning of the decade. While many of these women were restricted to “pink-collar” jobs—secretaries, stenographers, and telephone operators—the ability to earn a paycheck provided a level of autonomy that their mothers had never known. The “Sunday Drive” in a Ford Model T provided a mobile, private space that further eroded the traditional “chaperonage” system, allowing young people a degree of social freedom that deeply alarmed their elders in rural communities.

SECTION 13.4 · PART 2Prohibition: The “Noble Experiment” and the Rise of the Underworld

While young urbanites were redefining social norms, a different kind of moral struggle was unfolding over the consumption of alcohol. In January 1920, the 18th Amendment officially went into effect, making the manufacture, sale, and transportation of intoxicating liquors illegal throughout the United States. This monumental shift, known as Prohibition, was the culmination of a century of activism by the temperance movement. Supporters, often referred to as “dries,” were predominantly rural, Protestant, and motivated by the belief that alcohol was the root cause of most social ills, including poverty, domestic violence, and workplace accidents. They viewed the “Noble Experiment” as a way to “cleanse” the nation and assimilate the growing urban immigrant populations into a traditional American moral framework.

The federal government passed the Volstead Act to provide the necessary legal machinery for the 18th Amendment’s enforcement. However, the law faced immediate and widespread defiance in America’s large cities. Millions of citizens, particularly those in the urban North and East with large Catholic and Jewish populations, did not view drinking as a moral failing but as a standard part of their cultural and social traditions. The sheer scale of the demand for alcohol, combined with the government’s failure to provide adequate funding or manpower for enforcement, created a massive black market that transformed the American legal and criminal landscape.

Because the law only prohibited the sale and manufacture of alcohol—not its consumption—clever citizens found numerous ways to circumvent the ban. Doctors could prescribe “medicinal” alcohol, leading to a suspicious boom in prescriptions for everything from anxiety to the common cold. Religious leaders were permitted to use wine for sacramental purposes, which saw a sudden rise in “rabbis” and “priests” who had never previously led a congregation. For the average urban dweller, the primary destination for a drink was the Speakeasies. These were illegal, underground bars that flourished in every major city, often hidden behind the facades of legitimate businesses. To enter, a patron often needed to whisper a password or present a card, creating a culture of shared secrecy and rebellion against federal authority.

SECTION 13.4 · PART 3Nativism and the Closing of the Golden Door

The cultural tensions of the 1920s were deeply intertwined with a resurgence of Nativism—a powerful prejudice against foreign-born people. This sentiment was fueled by the “Post-War Shock” of 1919 and a growing fear that “New Immigrants” from Southern and Eastern Europe were bringing radical political ideologies like communism and anarchism to American shores. Many traditionalists believed that these immigrants, who were largely Catholic or Jewish, would never truly assimilate into the nation’s Protestant culture. This climate of suspicion was brought into sharp focus by the trial of Sacco and Vanzetti.

Nicola Sacco and Bartolomeo Vanzetti were two Italian immigrants and self-proclaimed anarchists who were arrested in 1920 for a payroll robbery and double murder in South Braintree, Massachusetts. The evidence against them was remarkably weak; several witnesses provided alibis for both men, and the physical evidence was inconclusive. However, the trial took place at the height of the Red Scare, and the judge was openly hostile toward the defendants’ radical beliefs and their “foreign” status. Despite international protests and multiple appeals, the two men were executed in 1927. To many, their death was a “judicial murder” that proved the American legal system had been corrupted by xenophobia. For nativists, however, the execution was a necessary defense of the American social order against the threat of radical subversion.

The political response to this nativist sentiment was a series of laws that ended the era of open immigration that had characterized the 19th century. In 1921, Congress passed the Emergency Quota Act of 1921, which established a temporary system of quotas based on the 1910 census. This was followed by the even more restrictive National Origins Act of 1924. This law set a permanent quota system, limiting the number of immigrants from any nation to 2 percent of the number of people of that nationality living in the U.S. as of the 1890 census. The choice of 1890 was deliberate; it predated the massive wave of immigration from Southern and Eastern Europe, thereby ensuring that the largest quotas would go to “old” immigrant groups from Northern and Western Europe (like the British and Germans). The 1924 act also completely barred all immigration from East Asia, a provision that deeply insulted Japan and poisoned diplomatic relations for decades to come.

SECTION 13.4 · PART 4The Battle for the American Mind: The Scopes Trial

The ultimate collision between the forces of modernism and traditionalism occurred in the sweltering heat of a courtroom in Dayton, Tennessee, in July 1925. This event, officially known as The State of Tennessee v. At the heart of the case was the struggle over the role of religion in public education and the validity of modern scientific theory in a society built on faith.

The conflict began with the rise of Fundamentalism, a Protestant religious movement that insisted on a literal interpretation of the Bible. Fundamentalists were deeply alarmed by the growing influence of “modernism”—the attempt by some theologians to reconcile religious faith with the findings of modern science and archaeology. They were particularly hostile to Charles Darwin’s theory of evolution, which they believed directly contradicted the story of creation in the Book of Genesis and stripped human life of its divine purpose. In 1925, several Southern states passed laws making it illegal to teach evolution in public schools. In Tennessee, this was known as the Butler Act.

The American Civil Liberties Union (ACLU) sought to challenge the law, viewing it as a violation of free speech and the constitutional separation of church and state. They recruited John Scopes, a young, well-liked high school biology teacher in Dayton, to intentionally violate the law. The trial that followed was less about Scopes’ personal guilt—he admitted to the “crime”—and more about a massive public “war of ideas” that was broadcast to the nation via the new medium of radio.

SECTION 13.5 · PART 1The Great Migration: Roots of a Demographic Revolution

The cultural phenomenon known as the Harlem Renaissance did not emerge from a vacuum. It was the direct result of one of the most significant internal movements of people in American history: The Great Migration. Between 1910 and 1930, approximately 1.5 million African Americans abandoned the rural South for the industrial cities of the North and Midwest. This movement was driven by a complex interplay of “push” factors—conditions that made life in the South increasingly intolerable—and “pull” factors—opportunities in the North that promised a better future.

The “push” from the South was rooted in the oppressive system of Jim Crow. Following the end of Reconstruction, Southern states had implemented a rigid system of racial segregation and disenfranchisement. African Americans faced constant threats of violence, including lynching, and were trapped in a cycle of poverty through sharecropping and debt peonage. Furthermore, a natural disaster in the form of the boll weevil—a beetle that decimated cotton crops—destroyed the economic livelihood of thousands of Black farmers. The desire to escape the “racial cast” system of the South and find safety for their families was a primary motivator for many migrants.

The “pull” toward the North was primarily economic. When World War I broke out in Europe, the flow of European immigrants to the United States stopped abruptly. At the same time, American factories were operating at maximum capacity to produce war materiel. This created a massive labor shortage in northern industrial hubs like Chicago, Detroit, Pittsburgh, and New York. Northern labor agents traveled through the South, offering free railroad passes and the promise of high-paying factory jobs. To a sharecropper earning pennies a day, the prospect of earning five dollars a day in a Detroit auto plant or a Chicago meatpacking house felt like a ticket to the promised land.

This concentration allowed for the cross-pollination of ideas and the creation of a sophisticated urban culture that challenged the racist caricatures of the past. However, the migration also led to increased racial tension in the North. As Black workers competed for jobs and housing with white ethnic groups, “sundown towns” and restrictive housing covenants became common, and the friction eventually boiled over into the violence of the Red Summer of 1919. Despite these challenges, the Great Migration fundamentally shifted the nation’s demographic center of gravity and provided the foundation for a new era of African American cultural assertion.

SECTION 13.5 · PART 2Harlem: The Laboratory of the “New Negro”

By the mid-1920s, Harlem had transcended its status as a mere residential neighborhood to become the symbolic capital of Black America. It was the heart of a cultural blossoming that contemporary observers called the “Negro Renaissance.” The movement was given its intellectual framework by Alain Locke, a Harvard-educated Rhodes Scholar and philosopher. In 1925, Locke edited a landmark anthology of essays, poetry, and art titled The New Negro. Locke argued that African Americans were undergoing a fundamental psychological transformation. They were no longer the “Old Negro”—the passive, submissive figure of the plantation era—but The New Negro: a self-assured, urban individual who took pride in their African heritage and demanded full social and political equality.

The “New Negro” philosophy emphasized the importance of self-expression and cultural achievement as tools for racial advancement. Locke and other leaders believed that if African Americans could demonstrate their genius in the arts and sciences, they would force white America to recognize their humanity and dismantle the barriers of prejudice. This belief turned Harlem into a magnet for the nation’s most talented Black individuals. Writers, painters, sculptors, and scholars flocked to the neighborhood, supported by a growing network of Black-owned businesses, newspapers like the New York Age, and civil rights organizations.

Key Concepts

The Social Geography of Harlem

Harlem’s vibrant street life was an essential part of the Renaissance. On any given night, the sidewalks were crowded with people dressed in the latest fashions, heading to “rent parties” (where people charged admission to a house party to pay their landlord) or world-class entertainment venues. The neighborhood was home to the Apollo Theater, the Savoy Ballroom, and hundreds of smaller jazz clubs and speakeasies. This environment fostered a sense of communal belonging and cultural pride that was unique in the American experience.

However, Harlem also reflected the paradoxes of the era. While it was a site of incredible creativity, it was also a place of poverty and overcrowding. Most Harlemites worked in low-wage service jobs, and the neighborhood’s infrastructure struggled to keep pace with the massive influx of migrants. Furthermore, Harlem’s nightlife became a destination for “slumming” white New Yorkers, who flocked to the neighborhood to experience the “exotic” culture of the jazz clubs. This dynamic created a complicated relationship between Black artists and white patrons, as the Renaissance was often dependent on white financial support and validation. Despite these contradictions, the spirit of Harlem in the 1920s represented a definitive break with the past and the birth of a modern African American identity.

SECTION 13.5 · PART 3A Literary Renaissance: Words as Weapons and Identity

Literature was perhaps the most enduring legacy of the Harlem Renaissance. A new generation of Black writers emerged, determined to tell the authentic story of the African American experience without the filter of white expectations. They used their work to explore themes of racial pride, the pain of discrimination, and the search for identity in a divided nation.

Langston Hughes was the most influential and prolific poet of the movement. Hughes’ work was revolutionary because it celebrated the lives of ordinary, working-class African Americans—the “low-down folks” who he believed possessed the most authentic culture. He incorporated the rhythms of jazz and blues into his poetry, creating a style that was uniquely modern and deeply rooted in the Black oral tradition. In his famous essay “The Negro Artist and the Racial Mountain,” Hughes declared that Black artists should not feel pressured to “paint like white people” but should embrace their own heritage with pride. His poems, such as “The Negro Speaks of Rivers” and “I, Too,” became anthems of racial endurance and hope.

While Hughes focused on the urban present, Zora Neale Hurston looked to the rural past. A trained anthropologist, Hurston traveled through the South collecting the folklore, songs, and stories of Black communities. Her novels, most notably Their Eyes Were Watching God, portrayed the rich, complex inner lives of African American women in a way that had never been seen before. Hurston’s work was often criticized by some male leaders of the movement for not being “political” enough, but her focus on the beauty and resilience of Black folk culture was a profound assertion of humanity in its own right.

Key Concepts

The Voice of Resistance

Other writers used their pens to express the militant spirit of the “New Negro.” Claude McKay, a Jamaican immigrant, wrote powerful, often angry poems that addressed the violence and injustice of the era. His sonnet “If We Must Die,” written in response to the Red Summer of 1919, urged African Americans to fight back against white mobs with dignity and courage. The poem was so resonant that it was later quoted by Winston Churchill during World War II. McKay’s work reflected the disillusionment of many Black veterans who had fought for democracy abroad only to find it denied to them at home.

The literary Renaissance was supported by key figures who acted as mentors and editors. James Weldon Johnson, a poet, lawyer, and executive secretary of the NAACP, worked to promote the work of young writers and was himself a significant literary figure with his collection God’s Trombones. These writers did not just create art; they created a new national narrative. They forced white readers to confront the realities of the Black experience and provided African Americans with a mirror in which they could see their own beauty, struggle, and intellectual power. By the end of the decade, African American literature had moved from the margins of the culture to the very center of the American literary canon.

SECTION 13.5 · PART 4The Sound of Freedom: Jazz, Blues, and the Cotton Club

If literature provided the intellectual heart of the Renaissance, music provided its pulse. The 1920s is famously known as the Jazz Age, and this era-defining sound was entirely the creation of African American musicians. Jazz originated in the multi-cultural environment of New Orleans, blending elements of ragtime, spirituals, and European classical music. As musicians moved North during the Great Migration, they brought this vibrant new sound to the urban centers, where it underwent a transformation into a sophisticated, modern art form.

The most iconic figure of this musical revolution was Louis Armstrong. A trumpet player and singer with a legendary sense of swing and improvisation, Armstrong transformed jazz from a collective ensemble music into a soloist’s art. His recordings with his “Hot Five” and “Hot Seven” groups set the standard for technical brilliance and emotional depth. Armstrong’s charismatic stage presence made him one of the first African American superstars, crossing the racial divide to become a beloved figure among both Black and white audiences.

At the same time, the “Big Band” sound was being perfected by composers like Duke Ellington. Ellington was a prolific songwriter and piano player who led a world-famous orchestra at Harlem’s Cotton Club. He elevated jazz to the level of high art, composing complex suites that captured the mood and energy of urban life. Ellington’s music was sophisticated and elegant, reflecting the aspirations of the “New Negro” movement. Meanwhile, the raw emotion of the African American experience was captured in the blues, led by “The Empress of the Blues,” Bessie Smith. Smith’s powerful voice and songs of heartbreak and resilience made her the most popular Black performer of the decade, providing a musical bridge between the rural South and the urban North.

Key Concepts

The Irony of the Cotton Club

The relationship between Black music and the white public was deeply complicated. The Cotton Club was the most famous nightclub in Harlem, featuring the finest Black musicians, dancers, and singers in the world. However, the club was owned by white gangsters and maintained a “whites-only” policy for its audience. Black residents of Harlem were barred from entering the very club that showcased their culture. This created a jarring irony: Black artists were celebrated as “exotic” entertainers for white tourists while being denied basic civil rights in the world outside the club’s doors.

Despite this exploitation, the popularity of jazz had a profound effect on American culture. It was the first truly American music to achieve global dominance, and its emphasis on improvisation and freedom mirrored the changing social values of the decade. Jazz provided a space where traditional boundaries were blurred; it was the music of the flapper and the speak-easy, the sound of a generation that was breaking free from the restrictions of the past. While it could not dismantle Jim Crow on its own, the “Jazz Age” forced white Americans to engage with African American creativity on a massive scale, laying the cultural groundwork for future social change.

SECTION 13.5 · PART 5The Politics of Identity: The NAACP and Marcus Garvey

The cultural energy of the Harlem Renaissance was matched by a fierce debate over the political future of the African American people. Two distinct visions emerged, representing the two major paths for racial advancement in the 20th century. One path emphasized legal struggle and integration into American society, while the other emphasized racial pride, economic self-reliance, and a retreat from white-dominated systems.

The NAACP (National Association for the Advancement of Colored People) represented the integrationist approach. Led by figures like James Weldon Johnson and W.E.B. Du Bois, the NAACP focused on using the legal system to challenge Jim Crow laws and fight for federal anti-lynching legislation. They published The Crisis, a magazine that showcased Black intellectual achievement and documented the horrors of racial violence. The NAACP’s strategy was based on the belief that African Americans should work within the American democratic system to claim their full rights as citizens. They sought to build alliances with white liberals and used the brilliance of the Renaissance as proof that Black people were ready for full inclusion in the national life.

Key Concepts

The Vision of Marcus Garvey

In sharp contrast to the NAACP was Marcus Garvey and his UNIA (Universal Negro Improvement Association). A charismatic immigrant from Jamaica, Garvey arrived in Harlem in 1916 and quickly built the largest mass movement in African American history. Garvey’s philosophy, known as Black Nationalism, was based on the belief that African Americans would never receive justice in a white-dominated society. He urged his followers to stop seeking integration and instead build their own independent institutions. “Up, you mighty race!” he famously declared. “You can accomplish what you will.”

Garvey’s UNIA focused on economic separatism, encouraging Black people to buy only from Black-owned businesses. He founded the Black Star Line, a steamship company intended to facilitate trade among Black people globally and eventually provide transportation for those who wished to return to Africa—a movement known as “Back to Africa.” Garvey’s message of racial pride and self-determination resonated deeply with the urban working class, who felt ignored by the more elite leaders of the NAACP. At its peak, the UNIA claimed millions of members worldwide.

Key Concepts

Clash and Collapse

The rivalry between Garvey and the NAACP was intense. Du Bois famously called Garvey “the most dangerous enemy of the Negro race,” fearing that his separatist message would undermine the struggle for civil rights. Garvey, in turn, dismissed the NAACP leaders as “opportunists” who were ashamed of their own race. The movement eventually collapsed after Garvey was targeted by the federal government (specifically J. Edgar Hoover’s anti-radical division). He was convicted of mail fraud in connection with the Black Star Line and was eventually deported to Jamaica in 1927.

While Garvey’s movement faded, his ideas remained a powerful force. He had instilled a sense of global racial consciousness and pride that transcended national borders. His emphasis on economic self-reliance and the beauty of Blackness would later influence the Black Power movement of the 1960s. The debate between the integrationist vision of the NAACP and the nationalist vision of Garvey defined the political landscape of the 1920s, reflecting the deep uncertainty and high stakes of a people who were determined to define their own destiny in a rapidly changing world.

AMERICAN EXPERIENCELetters from the South

The Historical Context

During the early years of the Great Migration, the Chicago Defender—the nation’s most influential Black newspaper—became a vital lifeline for those trapped in the South. The paper was often smuggled into Southern towns where it was read aloud in churches and barbershops. It carried advertisements for jobs in the North and published letters from desperate families seeking help to escape the Jim Crow South. These letters provide an intimate, “bottom-up” look at the human motivations behind this demographic revolution.

The Evidence: Voices from the Path

A Mother in Florida (1917): “I am writing to you for some information. I have a daughter 17 years old and I am a widow woman. I am trying to get to a place where I can make a living for us. The white people here are so mean and there is no work. Can you help us get a ticket to Chicago? I will work and pay it back. I just want a chance to be a woman.”

A Laborer in Alabama (1917): “I am a hard working man and I have a family of six. I have been working in the mines but the pay is so low we can’t buy shoes for the children. I see in your paper that there is plenty of work in the steel mills. I am not looking for a handout, I am looking for a job where a man is treated like a man and not a dog.”

Historical Significance

These letters reveal that the Great Migration was not just about “economics” in an abstract sense; it was about human dignity. The writers use the language of “manhood” and “womanhood” to describe their aspirations. They viewed the North not just as a place for higher wages, but as a place where they could escape the constant humiliation of segregation. The migration was a form of “voting with their feet,” a mass protest against the failure of the American South to protect the rights of its Black citizens. This movement of people created the urban environment that made the Harlem Renaissance possible, transforming individual desperation into a collective cultural triumph.
