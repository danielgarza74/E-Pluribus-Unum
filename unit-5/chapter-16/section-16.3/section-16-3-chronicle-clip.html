“Go for Broke”: The Rescue of the Lost Battalion

From “American Chronicles” in Chapter 16: World War II at Home

In late October 1944, deep in the Vosges Mountains of northeastern France, a desperate drama was unfolding. High on a ridge near the town of Biffontaine, 211 soldiers from the 141st Infantry Regiment—a National Guard unit from Texas—found themselves completely surrounded by German forces. They were trapped behind enemy lines with no food, little water, and dwindling ammunition. For five days, two other American units had attempted to break through the German “ring of fire” to rescue the Texans, but both had been beaten back with heavy losses. The situation for the “Lost Battalion” appeared hopeless.

To save the trapped men, the U.S. Army turned to the 442nd Regimental Combat Team. This unit was unique in the military: it was a segregated group composed of Nisei, second-generation Japanese Americans. While many of their families were still being held in Internment Camps like Manzanar back in the United States, these men were determined to prove their loyalty on the battlefields of Europe. Their motto was “Go for Broke,” a gambling term meaning to risk everything on one final, total effort.

The mission was a nightmare. The Vosges Mountains were covered in thick, dark forests and steep, muddy slopes. The weather was freezing, and a constant, bone-chilling rain turned the ground into a slush of ice and dirt. The German defenders were well-entrenched, holding the high ground and utilizing “tree bursts”—artillery shells designed to explode in the treetops, raining lethal wooden splinters and shrapnel down on the advancing Americans.

The Nisei soldiers fought for every inch of ground. Because the forest was too dense for tanks to be effective, the battle became a grueling series of hand-to-hand encounters. On the third day of the assault, the men of the 442nd reached a breaking point. Under heavy machine-gun fire, one company surged forward in a “banzai” style charge, screaming their battle cry as they climbed the slippery ridge. Despite staggering casualties, they refused to retreat. One Nisei soldier later recalled that they weren’t just fighting the Germans; they were fighting for the honor of their families and to prove that no barbed-wire fence could strip them of their American identity.

On October 30, after nearly a week of non-stop combat, the 442nd finally broke through the German lines. When the Nisei soldiers reached the exhausted Texas men of the Lost Battalion, the rescuers were met with disbelief. The Texans, many of whom were from small towns where they had never met a Japanese person, watched as these “enemy-looking” soldiers shared their meager rations and offered medical aid. The rescue was one of the most emotional moments of the war, a literal meeting of “the West” and “the East” under the American flag.

The cost of the rescue was devastating. To save 211 Texas soldiers, the 442nd suffered more than 800 casualties. In one company, only eight men remained standing out of the 186 who had started the mission. Despite these losses, the 442nd continued to fight, eventually becoming the most decorated unit in American military history for its size and length of service. When they returned to the United States, they were greeted not as “enemy aliens,” but as heroes who had sacrificed more for their country than anyone could have asked.

“‘Go for Broke’: The Rescue of the Lost Battalion,” E Pluribus Unum, Chapter 16, American Chronicles (closes Section 16.3).
