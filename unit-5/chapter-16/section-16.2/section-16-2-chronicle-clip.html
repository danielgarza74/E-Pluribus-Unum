The Invisible Army: The “Code Girls” of World War II

From “American Chronicles” in Chapter 16: World War II at Home

In the spring of 1942, as the “Arsenal of Democracy” began to roar to life in factories across the nation, a different kind of recruitment was taking place on the quiet campuses of women’s colleges. Young women majoring in mathematics, linguistics, and even music were being pulled aside by their professors for a mysterious interview. The questions were strange: Did they like crossword puzzles? Were they engaged to be married? Could they keep a secret—not just for a few weeks, but for the rest of their lives?

These women were the first recruits of a massive, top-secret effort that would eventually involve more than 10,000 female cryptographers. Known today as the “Code Girls,” they were the primary engine behind the Signal Intelligence Service and the Navy’s code-breaking units. While the Manhattan Project sought to build a weapon of physical destruction, these women were building a weapon of information. They arrived in Washington, D.C., by the thousands, taking up residence in crowded boarding houses and reporting to heavily guarded sites like Arlington Hall and the Navy’s headquarters on Nebraska Avenue.

The work was grueling and repetitive. In the days before electronic computers, “breaking a code” meant hours of manual pattern recognition. The women stared at endless strings of numbers and letters intercepted from Japanese and German radio transmissions. They were looking for the slight “breaks” in the encryption that would reveal a date, a set of coordinates, or the name of a ship. They worked in hot, windowless rooms filled with the clatter of “Bomba” machines and the smell of ink and tobacco smoke.

Despite the monotony, the stakes were life and death. The “Code Girls” were the ones who deciphered the intercepts of “Magic”—the code name for the broken Japanese diplomatic “Purple” code. Their work provided the intelligence that allowed Allied commanders to anticipate enemy movements. For example, during the Battle of Midway, intelligence gathered by these units allowed the U.S. Navy to surprise the Japanese fleet, a turning point that shifted the balance of power in the Pacific. In the Atlantic, they tracked the movements of German U-boats, providing the coordinates that allowed Liberty Ships to navigate safely through the “wolf packs” that had been sinking Allied supplies.

The most difficult part of the job, however, was the silence. The women were forbidden from telling anyone—including their parents or husbands—what they did. When asked about their work, they were instructed to say they were “secretaries” or “clerical workers.” This was not just a military rule; it was a matter of national security. If the Axis powers realized their codes had been broken, they would simply change them, blinding the Allied forces and potentially costing thousands of lives.

For decades after 1945, the “Code Girls” maintained their silence. While the soldiers returned to parades and the “Rosie the Riveters” were celebrated in posters, the women of the intelligence units faded into civilian life, their contributions known only to a few high-ranking officials. It was not until the late 20th century that the records were declassified, revealing that this “invisible army” of women had shortened the war by years and saved countless lives through the sheer power of their intellect and their ability to keep a secret.
