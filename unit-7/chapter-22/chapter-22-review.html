PART 3SECTION CHECKS · 1.1–1.4

Section Checkpoints

Choose the best answer. A short reason appears when you get one right.

22.1 Clinton won 43 percent of the 1992 vote, Bush 38 percent, and Perot 19 percent. What does Figure 22.1 suggest about voters?
Perot’s 19 percent was the strongest third-party showing since Theodore Roosevelt in 1912, and Clinton won with only 43 percent.
22.1 Why did the government shutdowns of 1995 and 1996 end up helping President Clinton?
Gingrich expected voters to blame the president. Instead Clinton looked like the reasonable negotiator, and Republicans were forced to pass a budget he could accept.
22.1 The Senate voted 45 to 55 on perjury and 50 to 50 on obstruction of justice. Why was Clinton not removed from office?
Neither charge came close to the 67 votes needed, so Clinton was acquitted and finished his second term.
22.1 Which statement best explains why Clinton signed the 1996 Welfare Reform Act despite protests from his own party?
The law fit his “New Democrat” centrism. Liberals warned it would push children into poverty, but Clinton said it would bring dignity and responsibility.
22.2 How did the U.S. mission in Somalia change, and what was the result?
Mission creep led to the October 1993 Battle of Mogadishu, where eighteen Americans died and Clinton pulled out all U.S. forces.
22.2 According to the chapter, how did the response to Kosovo in 1999 differ from the response to Bosnia?
Applying the lessons of Bosnia, the United States and NATO launched a 78-day bombing campaign without a declaration of war.
22.2 Why did the arrest of Timothy McVeigh shock the nation after the Oklahoma City bombing?
McVeigh was a white, native-born Gulf War veteran, which forced Americans to face homegrown terrorism.
22.2 What power did the World Trade Organization have that its predecessor, GATT, did not?
The WTO worked like a global trade court, which critics said eroded national sovereignty.
22.3 Why did the NASDAQ crash in the spring of 2000 after climbing past 5,000 points?
Many startups were burning through venture capital with no sustainable revenue, so panic set in and the bubble burst.
22.3 Which two forces did the chapter say drove deindustrialization?
Computers and robots reduced the need for workers, and globalization pushed companies to relocate to places with lower labor costs and looser rules.
22.3 Figure 22.13 shows why rural and poorer inner-city areas fell behind online. What was the main reason?
Cost and company investment decisions left these communities on slow dial-up or with no access, a gap called the digital divide.
22.4 Why did Nirvana’s success create a paradox for grunge?
Labels rushed to sign similar bands and designers sold pre-ripped “grunge” clothing on runways, troubling artists who valued authenticity.
22.4 Why did cable channels help create reality television?
Unscripted shows such as MTV’s The Real World needed no actors, sets, or scripts, so they were inexpensive to make.
22.4 What was the long-term result of the fight over Napster?
Federal courts ended Napster in 2001, but the technology was out of the bottle and eventually led to services such as iTunes and Spotify.

PART 5CASE STUDY · PRIMARY SOURCES

Big Government and Cyberspace

Read both sources, then answer in your own words.

“We know big government does not have all the answers. We know there’s not a program for every problem. We have worked to give the American people a smaller, less bureaucratic government in Washington. And we have to give the American people one that lives within its means. The era of big government is over. But we cannot go back to the time when our citizens were left to fend for themselves. Instead, we must go forward as one America, one nation working together to meet the challenges we face together. Self-reliance and teamwork are not opposing virtues; we must have both.”
President Bill Clinton, State of the Union Address, January 23, 1996.
“Governments of the Industrial World, you weary giants of flesh and steel, I come from Cyberspace, the new home of Mind. On behalf of the future, I ask you of the past to leave us alone. You are not welcome among us. You have no sovereignty where we gather. … You claim there are problems among us that you need to solve. You use this claim as an excuse to invade our precincts. Many of these problems don’t exist. Where there are real conflicts, where there are wrongs, we will identify them and address them by our means. We are forming our own Social Contract. This governance will arise according to the conditions of our world, not yours. Our world is different.”
John Perry Barlow, Electronic Frontier Foundation, February 8, 1996.
